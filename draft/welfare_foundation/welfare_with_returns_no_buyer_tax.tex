\section{Introduction}
%=============================================================================

Who bears a tax, and how much of it passes through into the price, is the
question of tax incidence \citep{weyl2013pass}. A platform's commission is one
example. Amazon deducts a referral fee from each sale, a commission of 15
percent of the sale price in many categories, so the seller receives the price
net of the fee. This commission works like a sales tax, and we call it a tax
from here on. Without returns, the pass-through and incidence of such a tax are
well studied in the literature. Online sales, however, are often returned, with
retailers expecting about one in five to come back.\footnote{National Retail
  Federation and Happy Returns, \emph{2025 Retail Returns Landscape}, October
  15, 2025, \url{https://nrf.com/research/2025-retail-returns-landscape}.
  The report estimates that 19.3 percent of online sales will be returned in
  2025.} On a kept sale the seller pays the whole tax. On a return the seller
refunds the price in full and gets most of the tax back, but Amazon keeps part
of it, at most 20 percent, or 3 percent of the price at a 15 percent
rate.\footnote{Amazon, \emph{Selling on Amazon pricing},
  \url{https://sell.amazon.com/pricing}, and \emph{North America selling fees},
  \url{https://sell.amazon.com.sg/north-america/pricing}. Amazon calls the
  amount it keeps the refund administration fee, the lesser of five dollars or
  20 percent of the referral fee.} Therefore the tax costs the seller different
amounts on a kept sale and on a returned one. How much of this cost the seller
can pass on to consumers depends on how purchases and returns respond to the
price, and on how costly it is for the seller to handle a return. It is not
clear how the pass-through and incidence change when the consumer's
return decision is taken into account, and whether the platform should
refund only part of the tax on a return.
We study this in a competitive market, taking the tax schedule as given. Each
buyer decides whether to buy and, after learning the fit, whether to keep the
good, and the tax reaches both decisions only through the price.

A tax creates a wedge between what the buyer will pay and what the good costs
the seller. Without returns, the wedge leads to underprovision on the purchase
margin, because some beneficial trades do not happen. Returns add a second
margin, the decision to keep the good, and a higher price can raise returns,
because buyers who remain need a better fit to keep the good, or lower them,
because the price changes who buys. It is therefore not clear whether the tax is
still costly to society, as it is without returns, or in which direction the
no-return results are biased when returns are ignored.

The paper asks who bears a tax that is partly refunded on returns and whether
it is socially costly, for a small change in the tax. It takes four steps.
First, how does the tax pass through into the price, and how do buyers and
sellers divide the change in their own surplus? A rise in production cost and a
rise in handling cost serve as benchmarks, since the first lowers the seller's
margin on every unit, the second only on returned units, and the tax lies
between the two. Second, counting the platform's revenue as part of social
surplus, does any loss to society come from the decision to buy or from the
decision to keep the good, and in which direction does that decision go wrong?
Third, what is missed by ignoring returns and using the no-return results for
this tax? Fourth, if the platform keeps a larger share of the tax on a refund,
does society gain or lose? The answers depend only on the return rate and on
how demand, supply, and returns respond to the price.

%=============================================================================
\section{Model}
%=============================================================================

We study the pass-through of a tax charged by the platform and of a
cost increase, such as a rise in return handling cost or in production
cost, in a homogeneous-good market under perfect competition with
product returns. The product sells at list price $p$. Consumers indexed by $i$ differ in
return hassle $\kappa_i$, the disutility of sending the good back, and in
the distribution $G_i$ of post-purchase match quality $u$. After buying,
type $i$ draws $u\sim G_i$. In the baseline model, the refund is restricted
to equal the list price, so the consumer's utility is $u-p$ when he keeps
the product and $-\kappa_i$ when he returns it.

Individual consumer surplus from buying at price $p$ is
\[
  cs_i(p)=\mathbb{E}_{u\sim G_i}\bigl[\max(u-p,\,-\kappa_i)\bigr].
\]
Consumer $i$'s purchase decision can be summarized by willingness to pay
$v_i$, defined as the price solving $cs_i(v_i)=0$. Type $i$ buys if and
only if $p\leq v_i$. Across types, $v_i$ has distribution $F$ with density
$f$, so gross demand is $D(p)=1-F(p)$.

Consumer $i$ returns the item with probability
$\phi_i(p)=\Pr(u<p-\kappa_i)$.
Averaging it over consumers with the same willingness to pay $v$ gives
$\phi(v,p)\equiv\mathbb{E}\bigl[\phi_i(p)\bigm|v_i=v\bigr]$.
The aggregate return rate $r(p)$ is derived by further taking the
average over consumers who make a purchase at $p$, equal to
\[
  \frac{1}{D(p)}\int_p^\infty \phi(v,p)\,dF(v).
\]
The return rate depends on how consumers purchase and on how each
purchaser returns. The price shapes the return rate through both.
Therefore, the effect of the price can be decomposed into a behavioral
channel and a selection channel,
\[
  r'(p)
  =
  \underbrace{
    \frac{1}{D(p)}\int_p^\infty \frac{\partial \phi(v,p)}{\partial p}\,dF(v)
  }_{\text{Behavioral channel}}
  +
  \underbrace{
    \frac{f(p)}{D(p)}\bigl(r(p)-\phi(p,p)\bigr)
  }_{\text{Selection channel}},
\]
The behavioral channel is the change in return probability holding the
purchase cutoff fixed. A higher price lowers the utility of keeping, so
purchasers return more often. The selection channel captures the change in
who buys. Its sign is undetermined without additional assumptions. In
general, this channel is non-positive because higher willingness to pay
lowers the return probability, so buyers at the margin return more often
than the average buyer and $\phi(p,p)\geq r(p)$.\footnote{As when
$u=v_i+\varepsilon$, with $\varepsilon$ independent of $v_i$ and return
hassle independent of $v_i$.} The two channels then move $r'(p)$ in opposite
directions. Below, $r'(p)$ is left unsigned.

Producer $j$ takes the list price $p$ and the
return rate $r$ as given and chooses quantity $q_j$ at cost $C_j(q_j)$, with
strictly increasing marginal cost. The platform charges an ad valorem
tax at rate $\tau$, so the seller keeps $(1-\tau)p$ on a kept unit. On a returned unit the seller refunds the full
price, so the buyer pays nothing. The seller pays net handling $h$ on
the good, which is gross handling cost minus scrap value, and a share $\beta\in[0,1]$ of the tax is retained by
the platform on each returned unit. The seller therefore
loses $\beta\tau p + h$ on that returned unit.\footnote{Amazon's refund
  administration fee is this rule for a referral fee.  On a return Amazon
  keeps the lesser of \$5 or 20 percent of the referral fee, and gives
  the rest back to the seller.  When the \$5 cap does not bind,
  $\beta=0.2$.}  We maintain $p+h>0$, which holds whenever scrap value
does not exceed list price. With return rate $r(p)$, the seller's
expected payoff per unit is the effective margin
\begin{equation}
  \mu^{s}(p,h,\tau,\beta)
  =
  \bigl(1-r(p)\bigr)(1-\tau)p - r(p)(\beta\tau p + h).
  \label{eq:mu-s}
\end{equation}

%=============================================================================
\subsection{Welfare measure}
%=============================================================================

Consumer surplus is the sum of individual consumer surplus over buyers who
purchase. A tax or a cost shock affects consumer surplus only through
the list price, and a higher list price changes it only through the
units that are kept. The effect of the price through the selection
channel is zero because a buyer at the purchase margin has zero
individual consumer surplus.
Its effect through the behavioral channel is also zero because keeping
and returning give the same utility at the keep threshold. What remains
is the direct effect of the price, and therefore
\begin{equation}
  CS'(p)=-D(p)\bigl(1-r(p)\bigr).
\end{equation}

Producer surplus is the sum of firm profits
$\mu^{s} q_j-C_j(q_j)$. A tax or a cost shock affects producer surplus
only through the effective margin, and by the envelope theorem a
one-dollar rise in the effective margin raises a firm's profit by the
quantity it chooses. Aggregating quantity supplied over firms gives
\begin{equation}
  \frac{dPS}{d\mu^{s}}=S(\mu^{s}),
\end{equation}
where $S(\mu^{s})$ is market supply.

%=============================================================================
\section{Pass-through}
%=============================================================================

We study pass-through of a production-cost increase, a handling-cost
increase, and the platform's ad valorem tax, and denote these pass-throughs
by the subscripts $c$, $h$, and $\tau$. For the production-cost increase, we
consider a parallel increase in marginal production cost, so marginal
production cost is $C_j'(q_j)+c$. These three shocks shift supply but not
demand, because consumers' purchase and return decisions respond only to
the list price $p$.

Pass-through $\rho_x\equiv dp/dx$, for $x\in\{c,h,\tau\}$, is determined by the
market clearing condition. Its general form is
\begin{equation}
  m(p,x)\equiv D(p)-S\bigl(\mu^{s}(p,x),x\bigr).
\end{equation}
Market clearing requires $m(p,x)=0$. All three pass-throughs are
evaluated at $\tau=0$. Differentiating $m=0$ gives
\begin{equation}
  \rho_x
  =
  \frac{-S'(\mu^{s})\,\mu^{s}_{x}-S_x}{S'(\mu^{s})\,\mu^{s}_{p}-D'(p)}.
  \label{eq:pass-through}
\end{equation}
Here $\mu^{s}_{p}$ and $\mu^{s}_{x}$ are partial derivatives of
$\mu^{s}(p,x)$, and $S_x$ is the direct shift of supply at a fixed
effective margin. The production-cost shock does not enter $\mu^{s}$, so
$\mu^{s}_{c}=0$, yet a unit increase in $c$ shifts supply as if $\mu^{s}$
fell by one unit, so $S_c=-S'(\mu^{s})$. The handling cost and the tax have no
direct effect on supply at a fixed effective margin, so $S_h=S_{\tau}=0$.
The handling cost reduces $\mu^{s}$ by $r(p)$, so $\mu^{s}_{h}=-r(p)$. The
tax reduces $\mu^{s}$ by $p\bigl[1-r(p)+\beta r(p)\bigr]$, so
$\mu^{s}_{\tau}=-p\bigl[1-r(p)+\beta r(p)\bigr]$. Substituting these into
\eqref{eq:pass-through} gives the three pass-throughs,
\begin{equation}
  \rho_c
  = \frac{S'(\mu^{s})}{S'(\mu^{s})\,\mu^{s}_{p}-D'(p)},
  \qquad
  \rho_h = r(p)\,\rho_c,
  \qquad
  \rho_{\tau} = p\bigl[1-r(p)+\beta r(p)\bigr]\,\rho_c,
  \label{eq:rho-links}
\end{equation}
In the classical model without returns, a unit tax rate and a unit
production-cost increase create the same per-dollar wedge, so
$\rho_{\tau}=p\,\rho_c$ differs from $\rho_c$ only by the ad valorem
scaling $p$. With returns, each shock shifts the supply curve to the left
by $S'(\mu^{s})$ times a scale factor. The factor is $1$ for production
cost, $r$ for handling cost, and $p\bigl[1-(1-\beta)r\bigr]$ for the tax.
Since supply and demand are locally linear, pass-through depends only
on how far the supply curve shifts. That shift is proportional to the
factor, so each pass-through is $\rho_c$ scaled by its factor. Since $\rho_h$ and $\rho_{\tau}$ are scalings of $\rho_c$, below we
focus on $\rho_c$. The signs of $\rho_h$ and $\rho_{\tau}$ are the same.

These pass-throughs take a more transparent form in price
elasticities, which also exposes the role of returns. Let
$\mu_p^0\equiv\mu^{s}_{p}\big|_{\tau=0}$ be the slope of the effective
margin with respect to price at zero tax. The demand and supply elasticities are
\[
\varepsilon_D(p)\equiv\frac{p D'(p)}{D(p)}<0,
\qquad
\varepsilon_S(p)\equiv S'(\mu)\,\mu_p^0\,\frac{p}{S(\mu)}.
\]
The supply elasticity $\varepsilon_S$ has the sign of $\mu_p^0$. With no
returns, $\mu_p^0=1$ and the supply elasticity is positive. With returns,
the supply elasticity can be negative if the return rate is an
increasing function of price. When the behavioral channel dominates, a
higher price raises the return rate, reduces the effective margin, and
lowers quantity supplied.

Multiplying numerator and denominator of $\rho_c$ by $p/S(\mu)$
gives the elasticity form,
\begin{equation}
  \rho_c
  =
  \frac{\varepsilon_S(p)/\mu_p^0}
       {-\varepsilon_D(p)+\varepsilon_S(p)}.
  \label{eq:rho_c_elasticity_final}
\end{equation}
The pass-through $\rho_c$ is the ratio of the excess-demand gap a
cost increase opens to the rate at which a price change closes it. A
production-cost increase reduces supply by $S'(\mu)$ and opens excess
demand of that size. An increase in the list price closes that gap at
rate $S'(\mu)\,\mu_p^0-D'(p)$. Compared to the classical no-return
formula, which sets $\mu_p^0=1$, the returns formula scales the
pass-through by $1/\mu_p^0$. When $\mu_p^0$ is above unity, one dollar
of list price restores more than one dollar of effective margin, so a
smaller price movement closes the gap. When $0<\mu_p^0<1$, the return
rate rises in price and one dollar of list price restores less than
one dollar of effective margin, so the price must move more and
pass-through is larger than the classical value. When $\mu_p^0$ is
negative, the effective margin decreases in the list price, and the
sign of pass-through depends on the magnitudes of the elasticities. In
particular, negative pass-through can happen when supply is more
elastic than demand in absolute value, so lowering the price raises
supply by more than it raises demand and clears the excess demand.
This is in sharp contrast to the model without returns, where
pass-through is always positive. The following two results summarize
the findings.
\begin{result}[Negative pass-through]
\label{res:negative-pt}
  We have $\rho_c<0$ if and only if $\varepsilon_S<0$ ($\mu_p^0<0$) and
  $|\varepsilon_S|>-\varepsilon_D$.
\end{result}
\begin{result}
\label{res:pt-bias-er}
  Relative to the correct returns-adjusted $\rho_c$, the classical
  no-return pass-through formula overestimates pass-through when
  $\mu_p^0>1$, agrees with it when $\mu_p^0=1$, and underestimates it
  when $0\leq\mu_p^0<1$.
\end{result}

Result~\ref{res:pt-bias-er} can be linked to the handling
cost and which channel of $r'(p)$ dominates.
The effective margin is likely to be above one when the selection
channel dominates and the handling cost is large, and to fall
below one when the behavioral channel dominates and the handling
cost is large. Differentiating the effective margin at $\tau=0$
gives $\mu_p^0=1-r(p)-(p+h)\,r'(p)$. A strong selection channel has $r'(p)<0$, so a higher price
lowers the return rate and saves handling cost, pushing
$\mu_p^0$ above one, whereas a strong behavioral channel has
$r'(p)>0$, so a higher price raises the return rate and adds
handling cost, pushing $\mu_p^0$ below one. A larger handling cost
amplifies both effects.

\section{Local incidence}
%=============================================================================

Having established how prices respond to different shocks, we turn to
local incidence, defined as the ratio of the change in consumer
surplus to the change in producer surplus for a small shock $x$. This ratio is the same for
the three shocks because the changes in consumer surplus and producer
surplus are both locally linear in how far the supply curve shifts,
and that distance cancels in the ratio. Let $Q\equiv D(p)$ and
$r\equiv r(p)$ denote the equilibrium gross quantity and the return
rate. The effect of a small shock $x$ on consumer surplus is
$Q(1-r)\rho_x$, which scales with $\rho_x$ and hence with the local supply shift.
For producer surplus, an increase in production cost has the same
impact as reducing the effective margin $\mu$ by one unit at a fixed
$p$, and the change in producer surplus is $Q\,d\mu/dx$, where $d\mu/dx$ scales with the local supply shift.

Figure~\ref{fig:cs_ps_mu_sign} shows consumer surplus, producer surplus, and
supply after a production-cost increase, when $\mu_p^0>0$ and when
$\mu_p^0<0$. Consumer surplus is the area between $D(p)$ and $D(p)r(p)$
above $p$, and producer surplus is the area between supply and $p$. The
left panel is a strong selection channel, so $\mu_p^0>0$, supply slopes
up, and the cost increase raises $p$. The right panel is a strong
behavioral channel, so $\mu_p^0<0$, supply slopes down, and the cost
increase lowers $p$.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{../../figuretable/output/cs_ps_mu_sign.png}
\caption{Consumer surplus, producer surplus, and supply after a
production-cost increase, when $\mu_p^0>0$ and when $\mu_p^0<0$.}
\label{fig:cs_ps_mu_sign}
\end{figure}

Consumer surplus changes by
\begin{equation}
  \frac{dCS}{dx} = -Q(1-r)\rho_x,
  \label{eq:CSx_incidence}
\end{equation}

Producer surplus is $PS(\mu,x)=\int_0^{\mu} S(z,x)\,dz$. Differentiating in
$x$ and using the equilibrium condition $S(\mu,x)=Q$ gives
\[
  \frac{dPS}{dx}
  = Q\frac{d\mu}{dx} + \Sigma_x,
\]
where $\Sigma_x\equiv\int_0^\mu (\partial S(z,x)/\partial x)\,dz$ is the
direct shift in the area under the supply curve. The chain rule is
$\frac{d\mu}{dx} = \mu_p^0 \rho_x + \mu_x$, so
\begin{equation}
  \frac{dPS}{dx} = Q(\mu_p^0 \rho_x + \mu_x) + \Sigma_x.
  \label{eq:PSx_incidence}
\end{equation}

The local incidence ratio is $I_x \equiv (dCS/dx)/(dPS/dx)$. For these three
shocks,
\[
  I_x = \frac{-Q(1-r)\rho_x}{Q(\mu_p^0 \rho_x + \mu_x) + \Sigma_x},
\]
and the rows of Table~\ref{tab:incidence_components} reduce to one expression
in $\rho_c$ and $\mu_p^0$.

\begin{result}
\label{res:incidence-ratio}
For production cost, handling cost, and the fee, the local incidence
ratio is identical:
\begin{equation}
  I_x = \frac{(1-r)\rho_c}{1-\mu_p^0 \rho_c}>0.
  \label{eq:incidence_result}
\end{equation}
\end{result}

The common ratio is positive. The denominator $1-\mu_p^0\rho_c$ in \eqref{eq:incidence_result} is the
per-unit producer loss on a one-dollar cost shock. Before the price
adjusts, producer surplus falls by $Q$. The list-price change moves the
effective margin by $\mu_p^0\rho_c$ per unit, and the envelope $dPS/d\mu=Q$
turns that movement into a recovery of $Q\mu_p^0\rho_c$. The net change in
producer surplus is $Q(\mu_p^0\rho_c-1)$, so the loss per unit is
$1-\mu_p^0\rho_c$. The numerator $(1-r)\rho_c$ is the per-unit consumer loss
on kept units. The incidence ratio compares these two per-unit burdens. When
the denominator is small and positive, consumers bear most of the loss.
When the denominator is negative, the recovery exceeds the direct cost and
producers gain.

Producer surplus rises with a production-cost increase exactly when
pass-through is negative. The recovery factor equals
$\mu_p^0\rho_c=\varepsilon_S/(\varepsilon_S-\varepsilon_D)$. It exceeds one if
and only if $\varepsilon_S<0$ and $|\varepsilon_S|>-\varepsilon_D$.
Result~\ref{res:negative-pt} states that this is the condition for
$\rho_c<0$.

When pass-through is negative, clearing requires the effective margin to
rise by more than one dollar. A one-dollar cost increase shifts the supply
curve upward and opens an excess-demand gap at the initial price. Quantity
supplied increases with the effective margin, so covering the original gap
requires $\mu$ to rise by one dollar. The price fall also raises quantity
demanded and opens a further gap. Supply must increase by more than the
original shift, so the effective margin must rise by more than one dollar.
Thus $\mu_p^0\rho_c>1$ is the market-clearing requirement when pass-through is
negative.

The incidence ratio is positive, so consumer and producer surplus move
together. When $\rho_c<0$, an increase in production cost, handling cost, or
the fee lowers the list price. Consumer surplus rises, and producer
surplus rises as well. When $\rho_c>0$, all three shocks reduce both
surpluses. In the classical model, $\mu_p^0=1$ and pass-through is positive,
so both surpluses fall.

\subsection{First-order private surplus and deadweight loss}
\label{sec:DWL}

Private surplus is the sum of consumer and producer surplus,
$W\equiv CS+PS$. Adding (\ref{eq:CSx_incidence}) and
(\ref{eq:PSx_incidence}) gives
\[
  \frac{dW}{dx}
  = -Q(1-r)\rho_x
    + Q(\mu_p^0\rho_x + \mu_x)
    + \Sigma_x
  = Q\bigl[\mu_x + \bigl(\mu_p^0-(1-r)\bigr)\rho_x\bigr]
    + \Sigma_x.
\]
Substituting $\mu_p^0=1-r-r'(p)(p+h)$ gives
\begin{equation}
  \frac{dW}{dx}
  = Q\bigl[\mu_x - r'(p)(p+h)\rho_x\bigr] + \Sigma_x,
  \label{eq:DWL_incidence}
\end{equation}
with $\mu_x$ and $\Sigma_x$ as in Table~\ref{tab:incidence_components}.
The term $-Q\,r'(p)(p+h)\rho_x$ is the first-order effect of the
price-induced change in the return rate. A change $r'(p)\rho_x$ in the
return rate costs $p+h$ per unit sold. The term is zero when $r'=0$.

With no returns, a unit production-cost increase lowers private surplus by
$Q$. In that benchmark $r=0$ and $r'=0$, so \eqref{eq:DWL_incidence} reduces
to
\[
  \frac{dW}{dx} = Q\,\mu_x + \Sigma_x.
\]
A unit production-cost increase shifts the supply curve up by one dollar at
every quantity. The private-surplus loss is a rectangle of height one and
width $Q$. Private surplus in that benchmark is $W=CS(p)+PS(p-c)$.
Differentiating, the price terms $-Q\rho_c$ and $+Q\rho_c$ cancel, so
$dW/dc=-Q$.

For an ad valorem tax at a zero rate and with no returns, $r=0$, so $\beta$
drops out. The same cancellation gives $dW/d\tau=-pQ$. One percentage point
is a wedge of $p$ dollars on every unit, and the price-change terms cancel.
Platform revenue $G=\tau p Q$ is a transfer. At $\tau=0$,
$dV/d\tau=dW/d\tau+pQ=0$. A one-dollar cost shock destroys $Q$ dollars of
private surplus. An infinitesimal fee moves $pQ$ dollars from private
surplus to the platform, so social surplus is unchanged at first order.

With returns, the return-rate term in \eqref{eq:DWL_incidence} is generally
nonzero for a production-cost increase, a handling-cost increase, and the
the fee. Private surplus then changes through the keep-or-return margin as
well as through the direct wedge. For the fee, platform revenue is still a
transfer. It is added back below, with
$G=\tau p\bigl[1-(1-\beta)r\bigr]Q$.

Substituting $\mu_x$ and $\Sigma_x$ from
Table~\ref{tab:incidence_components} into \eqref{eq:DWL_incidence}, and using
the pass-through links in \eqref{eq:rho-links}, gives the following
derivatives. The tax derivative is evaluated at a zero rate.
\begin{align}
  \frac{dW}{dc}
  &= -Q\,\Lambda,
  &
  \frac{dW}{dh}
  &= -Q\,r\,\Lambda,
  &
  \frac{dW}{d\tau}
  &= -p\bigl[1-(1-\beta)r\bigr]Q\,\Lambda,
  \label{eq:DWL_shocks}
\end{align}
where
\begin{equation}
  \Lambda \equiv 1 + r'(p)(p+h)\,\rho_c.
  \label{eq:Lambda}
\end{equation}

The unit term in $\Lambda$ is the direct wedge on units already sold. For a
production-cost shock that wedge is one dollar. For the fee it is
$p\bigl[1-(1-\beta)r\bigr]$ dollars. List-price terms cancel, so the change
in the number of buyers does not enter at first order. The term
$r'(p)(p+h)\rho_c$ is the further change on the keep-or-return margin after
the price moves. Each change $r'(p)\rho_c$ in the return rate costs $p+h$
per unit sold. A handling-cost shock multiplies the production-cost
derivative by $r$.

Freeze the aggregate return rate at its initial value $r_0\equiv r(p_0)$.
In that ex-ante economy the effective margin is
$\mu^{s}(p,h,0,\beta)$ with $r(p)$ replaced by the constant $r_0$,
\begin{equation}
  \mu^{\mathrm{EA}}(p)\equiv p-r_0(p+h).
  \label{eq:mu-EA}
\end{equation}
Applying \eqref{eq:DWL_incidence} to the production-cost shock in this
economy yields $dW/dc\big|_{\mathrm{EA}}=-Q$, the direct cost on units sold
with the return rate held at $r_0$. The ex-post piece is the residual
$dW/dc\big|_{\mathrm{EP}}\equiv dW/dc-dW/dc\big|_{\mathrm{EA}}$. Since
$dW/dc=-Q\Lambda$,
\begin{equation}
  \frac{dW}{dc}\Big|_{\mathrm{EP}}
  =-Q\,r'(p)(p+h)\rho_c.
  \label{eq:DWL-EP}
\end{equation}
For the fee at a zero rate, both pieces are multiplied by
$p\bigl[1-(1-\beta)r\bigr]$. The ex-ante piece is
$-p\bigl[1-(1-\beta)r\bigr]Q$. The ex-post piece is
$-p\bigl[1-(1-\beta)r\bigr]Q\,r'(p)(p+h)\rho_c$.
\begin{result}[Ex-ante and ex-post decomposition]
\label{res:EA-EP}
  For a unit production-cost shock,
  \begin{equation}
    \frac{dW}{dc}
    =-Q
    -Q\,r'(p)(p+h)\rho_c.
    \label{eq:DWL-decomp}
  \end{equation}
  The first term is the ex-ante change on the purchase margin.  The second is
  the ex-post change on the keep-or-return margin.  Each unit switched from keep
  to return costs $p+h$.  For the fee at a zero rate both terms
  are multiplied by $p\bigl[1-(1-\beta)r\bigr]$.  For a handling shock the two terms become $-Qr$ and
  $-Qr\,r'(p)(p+h)\rho_c$.
  The ex-ante term is the direct cost on units already sold. The change in the
  number of buyers does not enter at first order, so this term is not a loss
  of purchases.
  The ex-post term is a further loss when $r'(p)>0$ and $\rho_c>0$: the higher
  price raises the return rate, and there are too many returns.
  It is a gain, with private surplus still falling, when $r'(p)<0$: pass-through
  is then positive, the higher price lowers the return rate, and there are fewer
  returns than at the original price.
  It is a gain that exceeds the ex-ante loss when $\rho_c<0$: the price fall
  cuts returns, and private surplus rises.
\end{result}

Consumer and producer surplus move together, so $\Lambda$ has the same sign
as $\rho_c$. The no-return formula keeps only the ex-ante rectangle $-Q$.
The gap between that formula and $dW/dc=-Q\Lambda$ is the ex-post term
$-Q\,r'(p)(p+h)\rho_c$. That term can offset the rectangle, amplify it, or
reverse the sign of the private-surplus change.

When selection dominates, $r'(p)<0$. Then $\mu_p^0>0$ and hence $\rho_c>0$, so
a cost increase raises the list price. The higher price screens out frequent
returners and lowers the return rate. The ex-post term is then a gain, and
$0<\Lambda<1$. The private-surplus change stays negative, because
$\rho_c>0$ keeps consumer and producer surplus falling.

When $r'(p)>0$ and $\rho_c>0$, the higher list price raises the return rate.
Extra returns cost $p+h$ each, so the ex-post term amplifies the rectangle
and $\Lambda>1$. That amplification is larger when $r'(p)$ or $h$ is large.

When $\rho_c<0$, a production-cost, handling-cost, or fee increase
lowers the list price, so consumer surplus rises. The incidence ratio is
positive, so producer surplus rises as well, a Pareto improvement. Negative
pass-through requires $r'(p)>0$, so the fall in the list price lowers the
return rate. Each unit that switches from return to keep saves $p+h$, and
that saving more than offsets the direct wedge. Private surplus rises by
$Q|\Lambda|$ under the cost shock and by
$p\bigl[1-(1-\beta)r\bigr]Q|\Lambda|$ under the fee, with $\Lambda<0$. The
gain is larger when $r'(p)(p+h)\,|\rho_c|$ is large.
Result~\ref{res:welfare-bias} records these three comparisons.
\begin{result}[No-return private-surplus bias]
\label{res:welfare-bias}
  Consider a unit production-cost increase.  Relative to the
  correct returns-adjusted change $dW/dc=-Q\Lambda$, the no-return formula
  $dW/dc=-Q$
  \begin{enumerate}
  \item[(i)] overestimates the private-surplus loss if and only if
  $r'(p)<0$ (which implies $\mu_p^0>0$ and hence $\rho_c>0$, so
  $0<\Lambda<1$);
  \item[(ii)] underestimates the private-surplus loss if and only if
  $r'(p)>0$ and $\rho_c>0$ (hence $\Lambda>1$);
  \item[(iii)] overestimates the private-surplus loss with the wrong
  sign---reporting a loss of $Q$ when the shock in fact raises private
  surplus by $Q|\Lambda|$---if and only if $r'(p)>0$ and $\rho_c<0$.
  \end{enumerate}
  For the fee at a zero rate the same three comparisons apply to the ex-ante
  benchmark $-p\bigl[1-(1-\beta)r\bigr]Q$, which holds the return rate fixed.
  A formula that also sets the return rate to zero uses $-pQ$. The two agree
  when $\beta=1$ or $r=0$. When $\beta<1$ and $r>0$, $-pQ$ is a larger private
  loss, because the ex-ante benchmark gives the seller back the share
  $1-\beta$ of the fee on each returned unit.
\end{result}

Social surplus adds back the revenue the platform keeps.
On a kept unit that revenue is $\tau p$. On a returned unit it is the share
$\beta\tau p$. Expected revenue per unit is therefore
$\tau p\bigl[1-(1-\beta)r\bigr]$, and
\[
  G=\tau p\bigl[1-(1-\beta)r\bigr]Q,
\]
with $Q$ the equilibrium gross quantity. Write $V=W+G$. Differentiating
at $\tau=0$ gives $dG/d\tau=p\bigl[1-(1-\beta)r\bigr]Q$, so
$dV/d\tau=dW/d\tau+p\bigl[1-(1-\beta)r\bigr]Q$. Using \eqref{eq:DWL_shocks} gives the change in social surplus.
\begin{result}[Social surplus]
\label{res:social}
At a zero rate,
\begin{equation}
  \frac{dV}{d\tau}
  = -p\bigl[1-(1-\beta)r\bigr]Q(\Lambda-1)
  = -p\bigl[1-(1-\beta)r\bigr]Q\,r'(p)(p+h)\rho_c.
  \label{eq:DWL_V_tax}
\end{equation}
An infinitesimal fee raises social surplus when $r'(p)\rho_c<0$ and lowers
it when $r'(p)\rho_c>0$. The factor $p\bigl[1-(1-\beta)r\bigr]$ scales this
change, so a larger share of the fee kept on a refund enlarges the gain or
the loss.
\end{result}

%=============================================================================
\section{Global Incidence}
\label{sec:global-incidence}
%=============================================================================

The fee row of Table~\ref{tab:incidence_components} is the derivative at a
zero rate. It is not the integrand for a finite change in $\tau$.

A finite production-cost or handling-cost change integrates that shock's row
in the same table, with the row re-evaluated at the current equilibrium.

Along such a path, the global incidence ratio
$I_{x_0}^{x_1}\equiv \Delta CS/\Delta PS$, when $\Delta PS\neq 0$, is the
weighted average of the local ratio implied by $dCS/dx = I(x)\,dPS/dx$,
\begin{equation}
I_{x_0}^{x_1} = \int_{x_0}^{x_1} I(x)\,\omega(x)\,dx,
\label{eq:global-incidence-final}
\end{equation}
where $\omega(x)\equiv (dPS/dx)/\Delta PS$.

The global ratio can be negative, even though $I(x)$ is positive at every
point on the path. If $dPS/dx$ changes sign along the path, the weights
$\omega(x)$ take both signs, and the weighted average of positive local
ratios can be negative. A finite production-cost or handling-cost change
therefore need not preserve the sign of local incidence.

\appendix
\section{Welfare bias in primitives}
\label{app:welfare-bias-primitives}
Result~\ref{res:welfare-bias} has an equivalent statement in elasticities.
Recall $\varepsilon_r(p)\equiv p\,r'(p)/r(p)$ and
$\mu_p^0=1-r(p)-r'(p)(p+h)$. Then
$r'(p)(p+h)=\varepsilon_r(p)\,r(p)\,(p+h)/p$, so
\begin{equation}
  \mu_p^0>0
    \;\iff\;
  \varepsilon_r(p)
  <
  \frac{p}{p+h}\,\frac{1-r(p)}{r(p)},
  \label{eq:app-mu-er}
\end{equation}

In these elasticities, Result~\ref{res:welfare-bias} reads as follows.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(i)] overestimation iff $\varepsilon_r(p)<0$;
\item[(ii)] underestimation iff $\varepsilon_r(p)>0$ and either $\mu_p^0\geq 0$,
or both $\mu_p^0<0$ and $|\varepsilon_S|< -\varepsilon_D$;
\item[(iii)] overestimation with wrong sign (predicted loss, actual gain)
iff $\mu_p^0<0$ and $|\varepsilon_S|>-\varepsilon_D$.
\end{enumerate}
\begin{proof}
By Result~\ref{res:welfare-bias}, overestimation with $0<\Lambda<1$ is
$r'<0$, underestimation is $r'>0$ with $\rho_c>0$, and overestimation with
wrong sign is $r'>0$ with $\rho_c<0$.
Since $\varepsilon_r$ has the same sign as $r'$, write these in
$\varepsilon_r$.  For~(i), $\varepsilon_r<0$ forces $\mu_p^0>1-r>0$ and hence
$\rho_c>0$, so $0<\Lambda<1$.
When $\mu_p^0=0$ and $r(p)<1$, the derivative $r'(p)$ is positive,
$\varepsilon_S=0$, and $\rho_c=S'(\mu)/(-D'(p))>0$, so that case is in~(ii).
Result~\ref{res:negative-pt} translates the sign of $\rho_c$ into conditions
on $\mu_p^0$ and $|\varepsilon_S|\gtrless -\varepsilon_D$: with
$\varepsilon_r>0$ this is~(ii), and $\rho_c<0$ is~(iii).
\end{proof}
