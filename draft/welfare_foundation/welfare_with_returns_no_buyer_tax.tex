\section{Introduction}
%=============================================================================

Who bears a tax, and how much of it passes through into the price, is the
question of tax incidence \citep{weyl2013pass}. A sales tax and a platform's
referral fee are examples. The pass-through and incidence of such taxes are
well studied when purchases are final. Online purchases, however, are often
returned, with retailers expecting about one in five online sales to come
back.\footnote{National Retail
  Federation and Happy Returns, \emph{2025 Retail Returns Landscape}, October
  15, 2025, \url{https://nrf.com/research/2025-retail-returns-landscape}.
  The report estimates that 19.3 percent of online sales will be returned in
  2025.} Because the seller keeps the price only when the buyer keeps the good,
kept sales must cover not only their own tax and production cost, but also the
production, handling, and unrecovered tax costs of returned units. Recovering costs across fewer kept sales can make pass-through stronger. Yet a
higher price can raise the return rate, by making keeping less attractive
relative to a full refund, or lower it, by screening out buyers who return
more often. Because the return rate feeds back into the seller's expected
payoff per unit, quantity supplied, and the market-clearing price, it is
unclear whether pass-through with returns is stronger, weaker, or even
negative. The same question applies to production cost, incurred on every
sale, and return handling cost, incurred only on returns.

In practice, the tax on a sale also depends on whether the product is kept or
returned. When a purchase is returned, a government sales tax is refunded in
full, whereas a platform's referral fee is refunded to the seller only in
part. Amazon deducts the fee from each sale, 15 percent of the sale price in
many categories, and keeps up to 20 percent of it on a return.\footnote{Amazon,
  \emph{Selling on Amazon pricing},
  \url{https://sell.amazon.com/pricing}, and \emph{North America selling fees},
  \url{https://sell.amazon.com.sg/north-america/pricing}. Amazon calls the
  amount it keeps the refund administration fee, the lesser of five dollars or
  20 percent of the referral fee.} Platforms defend keeping part of the fee as
covering the cost of handling returns, yet that part is kept whenever a paid
order is refunded, even if the product never shipped. Whether the seller
should recover the tax in full on a return, or the platform should keep part
of it, is an open question.

Product returns also change how a tax affects welfare. When purchases are
final, an untaxed competitive market is efficient, and a tax lowers welfare by
deterring beneficial purchases. With full-refund returns, however, buyers do
not internalize the seller's production and handling costs on returned goods.
Through pass-through, a tax can raise or lower the market price, shifting both
total sales and the return rate. Since a price change can also raise or lower
the return rate, a tax may either reduce or increase costly returns while
shrinking or expanding sales. It is therefore unclear whether a tax, or
keeping part of it on returns, lowers welfare or raises it.

We study these questions in a competitive market where buyers receive a full
refund when they return a product. In this market, we analyze the
pass-through, incidence, and welfare effects of a unit tax that the platform
keeps in part on returns, as well as cost shocks such as production cost and
return handling cost. Buyers take the list price as given, choose whether to
buy, and after learning match quality choose whether to keep the good or
return it for a full refund at a return hassle. Because sellers cannot observe
individual return probabilities, buyers pool at one list price and every seller
faces the market return rate. Under perfect competition, each seller takes the
list price and the market return rate as given. These determine effective marginal revenue, the expected
net receipt per sale after return losses and taxes, and each seller chooses
only quantity by equating marginal production cost to effective marginal
revenue. Because buyers do not pay the tax or these costs directly, all three
shocks affect them only through the list price. A higher list price moves the return rate through two channels. The
behavioral channel raises returns among buyers who still purchase, while the
selection channel lowers the return rate if deterred marginal buyers return
more often than average and raises it if they return less often.

A rise in production cost, handling cost, or the tax shifts supply inward and
creates excess demand. Production cost hits every sale, handling cost hits
returned sales, and the tax hits kept sales in full and returned sales in
part. Their pass-throughs are therefore proportional to those per-sale shares.
How much the price must move depends on how strongly a one-dollar price
increase raises effective marginal revenue and thus quantity supplied. Without
returns, effective marginal revenue rises by a full dollar. With returns, the
seller collects that dollar only on kept sales, and the price change also
shifts the return rate. When the behavioral channel dominates, a higher price
raises returns and return losses, so effective marginal revenue rises by less
than one dollar. A higher price then expands supply by less than without
returns, so pass-through exceeds the no-return benchmark. When selection is
strong enough that saved return losses exceed the price gain missed on
returned sales, effective marginal revenue rises by more than one dollar. A
higher price then expands supply by more than without returns, so pass-through
falls below the benchmark. When behavior is so strong that extra return losses
outweigh the higher price on kept sales, a higher price lowers effective
marginal revenue and supply slopes downward. A lower price then increases both
supply and demand. Whenever supply is more elastic than demand in
absolute value, a lower price closes excess demand and pass-through turns
negative.

Local incidence is the ratio of consumer-surplus change to producer-surplus
change for a small shock. It is identical across production cost, handling
cost, and the tax because both the direct seller loss and pass-through scale
by the same per-sale factor. Local incidence is also always positive, so
consumer and producer surplus always move together. Under positive
pass-through, the higher price hurts buyers on kept units and lowers quantity
demanded. Because sellers supply less only when their net payoff per unit
falls, producer surplus also falls. Under negative pass-through, the lower
price helps buyers and raises quantity demanded. Because sellers supply more
only when their net payoff per unit rises, saved return losses must outweigh
the direct shock. Producer surplus therefore rises too, making a small shock
Pareto-improving.

Joint surplus is the sum of consumer and producer surplus. Without returns, or
at a fixed return rate, the price change on kept units is a pure transfer
between buyers and sellers, leaving only the direct cost of the shock on
existing sales. When the return rate changes with the price, consumer surplus
has no extra first-order change because marginal buyers had zero surplus and
switchers between keeping and returning were indifferent. Sellers, however,
lose the sale price plus handling cost minus any refunded tax on each extra
return. The standard no-return formula misses this seller return-cost effect.
When the selection channel dominates, pass-through is positive and the higher
price lowers the return rate. Saved return costs then partly offset the direct
loss, so the no-return formula overstates the joint-surplus loss. When the
behavioral channel dominates while pass-through remains positive, the higher
price raises the return rate. Extra return costs then add to the direct loss,
so the formula understates it. When the behavioral channel is strong enough to
turn pass-through negative, the lower price cuts returns enough that saved
return costs exceed the direct loss. Joint surplus then rises, and the
no-return formula gets the sign wrong.

Adding platform tax revenue to joint surplus gives social surplus. Tax
payments and tax refunds on existing sales are transfers between sellers and
the platform and cancel out, so a higher tax affects social surplus in only
two ways. First, at a fixed return rate, marginal sales generate zero consumer
surplus and zero seller profit, so a change in sales only affects the tax the
platform collects. Second, each extra return costs society the sale price plus
handling cost. When the selection channel dominates, a higher tax lowers both
the return rate and sales. Starting from zero, a small tax raises social
surplus. At positive taxes, it raises social surplus when saved return costs
outweigh lost tax revenue from fewer sales, and tends to lower social surplus
when the tax is high. When the behavioral channel dominates with positive
pass-through, a higher tax lowers sales and raises the return rate, so social
surplus falls. When behavior turns pass-through negative, a higher tax raises
sales and lowers the return rate, so social surplus rises. At any positive
tax, keeping a larger share of the tax on returns works like a higher tax.
Keeping more therefore raises social surplus when a higher tax does, while
letting the seller recover more raises it when a higher tax lowers it.

Finally, we show when the market overprovides or underprovides purchases. Two
wedges govern the purchase margin. First, a positive tax puts a gap between
what buyers pay and what sellers receive, pushing toward underprovision.
Second, because sellers cannot observe buyer return probabilities, they choose
quantity using the average return rate rather than the marginal buyer's return
rate. Without selection, only the tax wedge remains, so the market is efficient
at zero tax and underprovides at any positive tax. When marginal buyers return
more often than average, low-return buyers cross-subsidize them. The market
then overprovides purchases at zero tax, and at any positive tax where that
extra return cost exceeds the expected tax per sale. With positive
pass-through, a higher tax then raises the price and prevents these
inefficient purchases. When marginal buyers
return less often than average, the market underprovides even at zero tax.

\section{Model}
%=============================================================================

We study the pass-through of a tax charged by the platform and of a
cost increase, such as a rise in return handling cost or in production
cost, in a homogeneous-good market under perfect competition with
product returns. The product sells at list price $p$. Consumers indexed by $i$ differ in
return hassle $\kappa_i$, the disutility of sending the good back, and in
the distribution $G_i$ of post-purchase match quality $u$. After buying,
type $i$ draws $u\sim G_i$. In the baseline model, the refund is restricted
to equal the list price, so the consumer's utility is $u-p$ when he keeps
the product and $-\kappa_i$ when he returns it.

Individual consumer surplus from buying at price $p$ is
\[
  cs_i(p)=\mathbb{E}_{u\sim G_i}\bigl[\max(u-p,\,-\kappa_i)\bigr].
\]
Consumer $i$'s purchase decision can be summarized by willingness to pay
$v_i$, defined as the price solving $cs_i(v_i)=0$. Type $i$ buys if and
only if $p\leq v_i$. Across types, $v_i$ has distribution $F$ with density
$f$, so gross demand is $D(p)=1-F(p)$.

Consumer $i$ returns the item with probability
$\phi_i(p)=\Pr(u<p-\kappa_i)$.
Averaging it over consumers with the same willingness to pay $v$ gives
$\phi(v,p)\equiv\mathbb{E}\bigl[\phi_i(p)\bigm|v_i=v\bigr]$.
The aggregate return rate $r(p)$ is derived by further taking the
average over consumers who make a purchase at $p$, equal to
\[
  \frac{1}{D(p)}\int_p^\infty \phi(v,p)\,dF(v).
\]
The return rate depends on how consumers purchase and on how each
purchaser returns. The price shapes the return rate through both.
Therefore, the effect of the price can be decomposed into a behavioral
channel $r'_b(p)$ and a selection channel $r'_s(p)$,
\begin{equation}
  r'(p)
  =
  \underbrace{
    \frac{1}{D(p)}\int_p^\infty \frac{\partial \phi(v,p)}{\partial p}\,dF(v)
  }_{r'_b(p)}
  +
  \underbrace{
    \frac{f(p)}{D(p)}\bigl(r(p)-\phi(p,p)\bigr)
  }_{r'_s(p)}.
  \label{eq:r-prime-decomp}
\end{equation}
The behavioral channel is the change in return probability holding the
purchase cutoff fixed. A higher price lowers the utility of keeping, so
purchasers return more often. The selection channel captures the change in
who buys. Its sign is undetermined without additional assumptions. In
general, this channel is non-positive because higher willingness to pay
lowers the return probability, so buyers at the margin return more often
than the average buyer and $\phi(p,p)\geq r(p)$.\footnote{As when
$u=v_i+\varepsilon$, with $\varepsilon$ independent of $v_i$ and return
hassle independent of $v_i$.} The two channels then move $r'(p)$ in opposite
directions. Below, $r'(p)$ is left unsigned.

A consumer's match-quality distribution $G_i$ and return hassle $\kappa_i$ are
private information, so sellers cannot observe individual return probabilities
$\phi_i(p)$ or charge buyer-specific prices. All purchasing consumers therefore
pool at the single list price $p$, and each unit sold faces the market average
return rate $r(p)$. Under perfect competition, producer $j$ takes the list
price $p$ and the return rate $r$ as given and chooses quantity $q_j$ at cost
$C_j(q_j)$, with strictly increasing marginal cost. The platform charges a unit tax $t<p$,\footnote{We
use a unit tax rather than the platform's ad valorem referral fee. An ad
valorem tax interacts with the price inside effective marginal revenue even
when the return rate is fixed, and the pass-through formula is then hard to
interpret at a positive tax.} so
the seller receives $p-t$ on a kept unit. On a returned unit the seller
refunds the full price, so the buyer pays nothing. The seller pays net
handling $h$ on the good, which is gross handling cost minus scrap value,
and a share $\beta\in[0,1]$ of the tax is retained by the platform on each
returned unit. The seller therefore loses $\beta t+h$ on that returned unit.
We maintain $p+h-(1-\beta)t>0$. This is the seller's loss when a kept sale
is returned. With return rate $r(p)$, the seller's expected payoff per unit is
the effective marginal revenue
\begin{equation}
  \mu(p,h,t,\beta)
  =
  \bigl(1-r(p)\bigr)(p-t) - r(p)(\beta t + h).
  \label{eq:mu}
\end{equation}

%=============================================================================
\subsection{Welfare measure}
%=============================================================================

Consumer surplus is the sum of individual consumer surplus over buyers who
purchase. A tax or a cost shock affects consumer surplus only through
the list price. A higher price removes the marginal buyer, who had zero
surplus, so the lost purchase does not reduce consumer surplus. For an
inframarginal buyer, who still purchases, the utility of keeping falls by
the price increase. The utility of returning does not change, because the
higher price raises the payment and the refund by the same amount. The
return probability rises, but the buyer who switches from keeping to
returning was indifferent, so the switch does not reduce consumer
surplus. The remaining loss is one unit of utility on each unit that is
kept. There are $D(p)(1-r(p))$ such units, so
\begin{equation}
  CS'(p)=-D(p)\bigl(1-r(p)\bigr).
\end{equation}

Producer surplus is the sum of firm profits
$\mu q_j-C_j(q_j)$. A tax or a cost shock affects producer surplus
only through the effective marginal revenue, and by the envelope theorem a
one-dollar rise in the effective marginal revenue raises a firm's profit by the
quantity it chooses. Aggregating quantity supplied over firms gives
\begin{equation}
  \frac{dPS}{d\mu}=S(\mu),
\end{equation}
where $S(\mu)$ is market supply.

%=============================================================================
\section{Pass-through}
%=============================================================================

We study pass-through of a production-cost increase, a handling-cost
increase, and the platform's unit tax, and denote these pass-throughs
by the subscripts $c$, $h$, and $t$. For the production-cost increase, we
consider a parallel increase in marginal production cost, so marginal
production cost is $C_j'(q_j)+c$. These three shocks shift supply but not
demand, because consumers' purchase and return decisions respond only to
the list price $p$.

Pass-through $\rho_x\equiv dp/dx$, for $x\in\{c,h,t\}$, is determined by the
market clearing condition. Its general form is
\begin{equation}
  m(p,x)\equiv D(p)-S\bigl(\mu(p,x),x\bigr).
\end{equation}
Market clearing requires $m(p,x)=0$. Differentiating $m=0$ gives
\begin{equation}
  \rho_x
  =
  \frac{-S'(\mu)\,\mu_{x}-S_x}{S'(\mu)\,\mu_{p}-D'(p)}.
  \label{eq:pass-through}
\end{equation}
Here $\mu_{p}$ and $\mu_{x}$ are partial derivatives of
$\mu(p,x)$, and $S_x$ is the direct shift of supply at a fixed
effective marginal revenue. The production-cost shock does not enter $\mu$, so
$\mu_{c}=0$, yet a unit increase in $c$ shifts supply as if $\mu$
fell by one unit, so $S_c=-S'(\mu)$. The handling cost and the tax have no
direct effect on supply at a fixed effective marginal revenue, so $S_h=S_{t}=0$.
The handling cost reduces the effective marginal revenue when a consumer returns the
product, so $\mu_{h}=-r(p)$. The tax reduces effective marginal revenue by $1$ on a kept unit and by
$\beta$ on a returned unit, so $\mu_{t}=-\theta$ with
$\theta\equiv 1-r(p)+\beta r(p)$. This factor is positive because
$\theta=1-r+\beta r\geq 1-r>0$. Substituting these into
\eqref{eq:pass-through} gives the three pass-throughs,
\begin{equation}
  \rho_c
  = \frac{S'(\mu)}{S'(\mu)\,\mu_{p}-D'(p)},
  \qquad
  \rho_h = r(p)\,\rho_c,
  \qquad
  \rho_{t} = \theta\,\rho_c,
  \label{eq:rho-links}
\end{equation}
In the classical model without returns, a unit tax and a unit
production-cost increase create the same wedge, so
$\rho_{t}=\rho_c$. With returns, each shock shifts the supply curve to the left
by $S'(\mu)$ times a scale factor. The factor is $1$ for production
cost, $r$ for handling cost, and $\theta$ for the tax.
Since supply and demand are locally linear, pass-through depends only
on how far the supply curve shifts. That shift is proportional to the
factor, so each pass-through is $\rho_c$ scaled by its factor.

These pass-throughs take a more transparent form in price
elasticities, which also exposes the role of returns. The demand and supply elasticities are
\[
\varepsilon_D(p)\equiv\frac{p D'(p)}{D(p)}<0,
\qquad
\varepsilon_S(p)\equiv S'(\mu)\,\mu_p\,\frac{p}{S(\mu)}.
\]
The supply elasticity $\varepsilon_S$ has the sign of $\mu_p$, which can be
written explicitly as
\begin{equation}
  \mu_p=(1-r)-r'\bigl[p+h-(1-\beta)t\bigr].
  \label{eq:mu-p}
\end{equation}
With no returns, $r=0$ and $r'=0$, so $\mu_p=1$ and the supply elasticity is positive. With returns,
the supply elasticity can be negative. A higher price raises the return rate.
When the behavioral channel is strong enough, the higher price can reduce
effective marginal revenue and thus lower quantity supplied.

Multiplying numerator and denominator of $\rho_c$ by $p/S(\mu)$
gives the elasticity form,
\begin{equation}
  \rho_c
  =
  \frac{\varepsilon_S(p)/\mu_p}
       {-\varepsilon_D(p)+\varepsilon_S(p)}.
  \label{eq:rho_c_elasticity_final}
\end{equation}
The pass-through $\rho_c$ is the ratio of the excess-demand gap a
cost increase opens to the rate at which a price change closes it. A
production-cost increase reduces supply by $S'(\mu)$ and opens excess
demand of that size. An increase in the list price closes that gap at
rate $S'(\mu)\,\mu_p-D'(p)$. Compared to the classical no-return
formula, which sets $\mu_p=1$, the returns formula scales the
pass-through by $1/\mu_p$. When $\mu_p$ is above unity, one dollar
of list price restores more than one dollar of effective marginal revenue, so a
smaller price movement closes the gap. When $0<\mu_p<1$, the return
rate rises in price and one dollar of list price restores less than
one dollar of effective marginal revenue, so the price must move more and
pass-through is larger than the classical value. When $\mu_p$ is
negative, the effective marginal revenue decreases in the list price, and the
sign of pass-through depends on the magnitudes of the elasticities. In
particular, negative pass-through can happen when supply is more
elastic than demand in absolute value, so lowering the price raises
supply by more than it raises demand and clears the excess demand.
This is in sharp contrast to the model without returns, where
pass-through is always positive. The following two results summarize
the findings.
\begin{result}[Negative pass-through]
\label{res:negative-pt}
  We have $\rho_c<0$ if and only if $\varepsilon_S<0$ ($\mu_p<0$) and
  $|\varepsilon_S|>-\varepsilon_D$.
\end{result}
\begin{result}
\label{res:pt-bias-er}
  Relative to the correct returns-adjusted $\rho_c$, the classical
  no-return pass-through formula overestimates pass-through when
  $\mu_p>1$, agrees with it when $\mu_p=1$, and underestimates it
  when $0\leq\mu_p<1$.
\end{result}

Result~\ref{res:pt-bias-er} depends on which channel dominates the return
rate, on the net handling cost, and on the share $\beta$ of the tax kept
on a return. At $r'(p)=0$, $\mu_p=1-r(p)<1$ whenever $r(p)>0$, because a
one-dollar price increase is kept only on non-returned sales. When the
behavioral channel dominates, $r'(p)>0$, so a higher price raises the return
rate, adds return cost, and pushes $\mu_p$ further below $1-r(p)<1$. When the
selection channel dominates, $r'(p)<0$, so a higher price lowers the return
rate and raises $\mu_p$ above $1-r(p)$, exceeding one if and only if
$-r'(p)\bigl[p+h-(1-\beta)t\bigr]>r(p)$. A larger handling cost and a larger
retained tax share $\beta$ amplify both effects because each return is more
costly to the seller.

%=============================================================================
\section{Local incidence}
%=============================================================================

Having established how prices respond to different shocks, we turn to
local incidence, defined as the ratio of the change in consumer
surplus to the change in producer surplus for a small shock $x$. Before a
discussion of the incidence, it is useful to see the pass-through under
different primitives and to show consumer surplus and producer surplus
on the same graph.

Figure~\ref{fig:cs_ps_mu_sign} shows demand, the inverse supply curve,
consumer surplus, producer surplus, and how the inverse supply curve
moves after a production-cost increase. The inverse supply curve gives
the price required to supply a given quantity. Consumer surplus is the
area between gross demand $D(p)$ and returns $D(p)r(p)$ above $p$.
Producer surplus is the area to the left of the supply curve and below
$p$. For a unit production-cost increase, quantity
supplied decreases at a fixed price, and the reduction is the same as a
one-unit fall in the effective marginal revenue.

The left panel shows the case where the selection channel
is stronger than the behavioral channel, so that $r(p)$ falls with $p$. This
can be seen from the graph as returns $D(p)r(p)$ taking a smaller share of
gross demand $D(p)$ at a higher $p$. A higher $p$ screens out consumers with a high return probability,
so the effective marginal revenue improves and the seller supplies more,
as illustrated by the locally upward-sloping supply curve.
On that region, a unit production-cost increase shifts the
inverse supply curve inward at a given quantity, so the equilibrium
price rises.

The right panel shows the case where the behavioral channel
is stronger than the selection channel, so that $r(p)$ rises with $p$.
This can be seen from the graph as returns $D(p)r(p)$ taking a larger share
of gross demand $D(p)$ at a higher $p$. The inverse supply curve is hump-shaped.
It is not profitable to supply at a high $p$, because the return rate is so
high that almost any sale leads to a return. It is also not profitable to
supply at a low $p$, because the low profit margin is not enough to cover
the production cost. A higher price raises the return
probability for purchasers. The seller loses the price and the handling
cost on the additional returns. When this loss outweighs the extra revenue
on kept sales, the effective marginal revenue falls and the seller supplies
less, as illustrated by the locally downward-sloping supply curve.
On that region, a unit production-cost increase shifts the inverse supply curve
inward at a given quantity, so the equilibrium price falls.

\centering

\caption{Consumer surplus, producer surplus, and supply after a
production-cost increase.}
\label{fig:cs_ps_mu_sign}
\end{figure}

One observation is that the incidence is the same for the three shocks.
Intuitively, at a fixed price each shock reduces quantity supplied as if the
effective marginal revenue had fallen. The three falls differ only in magnitude. Supply and demand are locally
linear, so pass-through and the quantity change depend only on that magnitude.
The changes in consumer surplus and producer surplus are both locally linear
in how far the supply curve shifts, and that magnitude cancels in the ratio.
Mathematically, let $Q\equiv D(p)$ and
$r\equiv r(p)$ denote the equilibrium gross quantity and the return
rate. A shock $x$ affects consumer surplus only through the price, so
$\frac{dCS}{dx}=-Q(1-r)\rho_x$. This change scales with $\rho_x$ and hence
with the local supply shift.
Producer surplus, written as a function of $x$, is
\[
  PS(x)=\int_0^{\mu(p(x),x)} S(z,x)\,dz.
\]
A shock $x$ affects producer surplus through pass-through, through the direct
effect on the effective marginal revenue, holding the price fixed, and through a shift
of the supply curve, holding the price and effective marginal revenue fixed.
Differentiating in $x$ and using
$S\bigl(\mu(p(x),x),x\bigr)=Q$ gives
$dPS/dx=Q\,d\mu(p(x),x)/dx+\Sigma_x$, where
$\Sigma_x\equiv\int_0^{\mu(p(x),x)}(\partial S(z,x)/\partial x)\,dz$.
Because $d\mu(p(x),x)/dx=\mu_p\rho_x+\mu_x$,
\begin{equation}
  \frac{dPS}{dx} = Q\mu_p\rho_x + Q\mu_x + \Sigma_x.
  \label{eq:PSx_incidence}
\end{equation}
Table~\ref{tab:incidence_components} shows that the joint effect of these three
channels is also proportional to pass-through and to the degree of the local
supply shift.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering

The local incidence ratio is therefore the same for the three shocks. The next
result formalizes this and shows that the common ratio is positive.

\begin{result}
\label{res:incidence-ratio}
For production cost, handling cost, and the tax, the local incidence
ratio is identical and positive.
\begin{equation}
  I_x = \frac{(1-r)\rho_c}{1-\mu_p \rho_c}>0.
  \label{eq:incidence_result}
\end{equation}
\end{result}
\begin{proof}
Since $1-r>0$, the ratio is positive if $\rho_c$ has the same sign as
$1-\mu_p\rho_c$. Multiplying the pass-through formula
\eqref{eq:rho_c_elasticity_final} by $\mu_p$ gives
$\mu_p\rho_c=\varepsilon_S/(\varepsilon_S-\varepsilon_D)$.
This exceeds one if and only if $\varepsilon_S<0$ and $|\varepsilon_S|>-\varepsilon_D$.
Result~\ref{res:negative-pt} states that this is the necessary and sufficient
condition for $\rho_c<0$.
\end{proof}

For a unit increase in production cost, the numerator $(1-r)\rho_c$ is the
per-unit consumer loss on kept units. The denominator $1-\mu_p\rho_c$ is
the per-unit producer loss. Before the price adjusts, the direct effect
through the supply curve shift lowers producer surplus by $Q$. The indirect price
pass-through effect moves the effective marginal revenue by $\mu_p\rho_c$ per unit,
and the envelope $dPS/d\mu=Q$ restores producer surplus by $Q\mu_p\rho_c$.
Together, the net change in producer surplus is $Q(\mu_p\rho_c-1)$.

The incidence is positive even when pass-through is negative because the
recovery from the indirect price pass-through effect more than offsets the
direct loss from the supply curve shift. With negative pass-through, the
new equilibrium price is lower and quantity demanded is higher. Market clearing requires the new equilibrium
quantity supplied to exceed the original quantity. The price channel must
therefore raise quantity supplied by more than the initial decrease caused
by the inward shift of the supply curve. This implies that the price
channel raises the effective marginal revenue by more than one unit, so the
denominator is negative when pass-through is negative. The reasoning in
the opposite direction holds for positive pass-through.

The result has a striking implication that a production cost increase can be
Pareto-improving. The uniformly positive incidence ratio means consumer and
producer surplus always move together, regardless of the sign of
pass-through. When pass-through is negative, raising production cost,
handling cost, or the tax lowers the price, reduces return rates, and saves
enough handling costs to more than offset the direct resource drain. Both
consumers and producers benefit. When pass-through is positive, all three
cost shocks reduce both surpluses, as in the classical model, where $\mu_p=1$
and $\rho_c>0$.

%=============================================================================
\subsection{First-order effect on joint surplus and ex-ante ex-post decomposition}
\label{sec:private-surplus}
%=============================================================================

Joint surplus is $W\equiv CS+PS$. A unit increase in production cost
changes it by the sum of the consumer and producer changes in
Table~\ref{tab:incidence_components},
\begin{equation}
  \frac{dW}{dc}
  =
  -Q(1-r)\rho_c+Q(\mu_p\rho_c-1).
  \label{eq:dW-c-raw}
\end{equation}
Substituting $\mu_p$
into \eqref{eq:dW-c-raw} gives
\begin{equation}
  \frac{dW}{dc}=-Q\Lambda,
  \qquad
  \Lambda\equiv 1+r'(p)\rho_c\bigl[p+h-(1-\beta)t\bigr].
  \label{eq:Lambda}
\end{equation}
Pass-through of the production cost changes the price by $\rho_c$ on each
unit. Without returns, buyers lose $\rho_c$ on each of the $Q$ units sold, so
they lose $Q\rho_c$. Sellers gain $\rho_c$ on each of those units, so they
gain the same amount. These price effects cancel, so what remains is the
direct effect of the higher production cost on the equilibrium quantity
sold, a change of $-Q$.

With returns, suppose first that the aggregate return rate $r(p)$ does not
change with the price, so $r'(p)=0$ and $\Lambda=1$. Buyers lose $\rho_c$ on
each of the $Q(1-r)$ kept units and lose nothing on returned units because
the refund equals the price. Sellers gain the same $Q(1-r)\rho_c$ on kept
units. These price effects still cancel, leaving the direct production-cost
loss $-Q$, a rectangle of height one and width $Q$.

Allowing $r(p)$ to vary with the price adds no further first-order change in
consumer surplus. A marginal buyer who stops buying had zero consumer surplus,
and an inframarginal buyer who switches between keeping and returning was
indifferent between the two. On the seller side, however, each unit increase
in the aggregate return rate costs $p+h-(1-\beta)t$, because the seller gives
up the price $p$, pays net handling $h$, and recovers $(1-\beta)t$ of the
tax. By \eqref{eq:r-prime-decomp}, the price moves the aggregate return rate
by $r'(p)\rho_c$ through both inframarginal switches $r'_b(p)$ and marginal
buyer entry and exit $r'_s(p)$. Multiplying by $Q$ changes joint surplus by
$-Qr'(p)\rho_c\bigl[p+h-(1-\beta)t\bigr]$. Added to the direct loss $-Q$, the
total change is $-Q\Lambda$.

Handling cost scales this change by $r$, and the tax scales it by $\theta$.
\begin{equation}
  \frac{dW}{dh}=-Qr\Lambda,
  \qquad
  \frac{dW}{dt}=-Q\theta\Lambda.
  \label{eq:dW-shocks}
\end{equation}

Freezing the aggregate return rate at $r(p)$ defines the ex-ante and ex-post
decomposition of $dW/dx$. The ex-ante term is the change at $r'(p)=0$, equal
to the rectangles $-Q$, $-Qr$, and $-Q\theta$. The ex-post term is the
remaining change from the price response of the aggregate return rate, equal
to $-Q\,r'(p)\rho_c\bigl[p+h-(1-\beta)t\bigr]$ scaled by $1$, $r$, or
$\theta$.
\begin{result}[Ex-ante and ex-post decomposition]
\label{res:EA-EP}
\begin{align}
  \frac{dW}{dc}
  &=
  -Q-Q\,r'(p)\rho_c\bigl[p+h-(1-\beta)t\bigr],
  \label{eq:dW-decomp} \\
  \frac{dW}{dh}
  &=
  -Qr-Qr\,r'(p)\rho_c\bigl[p+h-(1-\beta)t\bigr], \\
  \frac{dW}{dt}
  &=
  -Q\theta-Q\theta\,r'(p)\rho_c\bigl[p+h-(1-\beta)t\bigr].
\end{align}
\end{result}

Because $dW/dc=-Q\Lambda$, the ex-post term offsets the ex-ante loss when
$0<\Lambda<1$, amplifies it when $\Lambda>1$, and reverses it into a net gain
when $\Lambda<0$. By Result~\ref{res:incidence-ratio}, consumer and producer
surplus move together, falling when $\rho_c>0$ and rising when $\rho_c<0$, so
$\Lambda$ shares the sign of $\rho_c$, while the ex-post term has the sign of
$-r'(p)\rho_c$. Because $r'(p)$ also enters $\rho_c$ through the supply slope,
pass-through flips sign at a single positive cutoff,
\begin{equation}
  \bar{r}'
  \equiv
  \frac{1-r(p)-D'(p)/S'(\mu)}{p+h-(1-\beta)t}
  >0,
  \label{eq:r-thresholds}
\end{equation}
with $\rho_c>0$ for $r'(p)<\bar{r}'$ and $\rho_c<0$ for $r'(p)>\bar{r}'$.

Since the ex-post term has the sign of $-r'(p)\rho_c$, zero and $\bar{r}'$
divide $r'(p)$ into three welfare regimes, and the ex-post term is not monotone
in $r'(p)$. When $r'(p)<0$, the selection channel dominates and pass-through is
positive. The higher list price screens out frequent returners, the return rate
falls, and seller return losses shrink. The ex-post term is then a gain that
cuts the joint-surplus loss from $Q$ to $Q\Lambda$, with $0<\Lambda<1$. When
$0<r'(p)<\bar{r}'$, the behavioral channel is intermediate and pass-through
stays positive.\footnote{From \eqref{eq:mu-p}, supply slopes up below
$r'_0\equiv\frac{1-r(p)}{p+h-(1-\beta)t}\in(0,\bar{r}')$ and slopes down above
$r'_0$, where saved return costs from a price cut outweigh lost revenue on kept
units. On $(r'_0,\bar{r}')$, supply remains less elastic than demand in
absolute value, so $\rho_c>0$.} The higher list price makes keeping less
attractive, the return rate rises, and seller return losses increase, raising
the loss to $Q\Lambda$ with $\Lambda>1$. When $r'(p)>\bar{r}'$, the behavioral
channel is strong enough that supply slopes down and is more elastic than
demand in absolute value. A lower list price then raises supply by more than
demand to clear excess demand, so pass-through turns negative. That price drop
makes keeping more attractive, lowers the return rate, and saves enough return
cost to outweigh the direct cost shock, so joint surplus rises by $Q|\Lambda|$
($\Lambda<0$). Across all three regimes, the ex-post term is larger in
magnitude when $|r'(p)|$, $|\rho_c|$, net handling $h$, or the retained tax
share $\beta$ is larger. Result~\ref{res:welfare-bias} summarizes the three
regimes and the resulting bias from using the ex-ante formula $-Q$, which
ignores how the return rate responds to the price.

\begin{result}[Ex-post term and no-return joint-surplus bias]
\label{res:welfare-bias}
\label{res:expost-r}
With $\bar{r}'>0$ defined in \eqref{eq:r-thresholds}, consider a unit
production-cost increase and compare $dW/dc=-Q\Lambda$ with the ex-ante
formula $-Q$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(i)] $0<\Lambda<1$ if and only if $r'(p)<0$. Then $\rho_c>0$, the
ex-post term is a gain, and $-Q$ overestimates the joint-surplus loss.
\item[(ii)] $\Lambda>1$ if and only if $0<r'(p)<\bar{r}'$ (equivalently,
$r'(p)>0$ and $\rho_c>0$). Then the ex-post term is a loss, and $-Q$
underestimates the joint-surplus loss.
\item[(iii)] $\Lambda<0$ if and only if $r'(p)>\bar{r}'$ (equivalently,
$r'(p)>0$ and $\rho_c<0$). Then the ex-post term is a gain that raises joint
surplus by $Q|\Lambda|$, and $-Q$ overestimates the loss with the wrong sign.
\end{enumerate}
For handling cost and the tax, the same cutoffs compare $-Qr\Lambda$ with
$-Qr$ and $-Q\theta\Lambda$ with $-Q\theta$.
\end{result}
\begin{proof}
Since $1-r>0$, $p+h-(1-\beta)t>0$, and $D'(p)/S'(\mu)<0$,
\eqref{eq:r-thresholds} gives $\bar{r}'>0$. Substituting \eqref{eq:mu-p} into
the denominator of $\rho_c$ in \eqref{eq:rho-links} gives
$S'(\mu)\mu_p-D'(p)=S'(\mu)(\bar{r}'-r')\bigl[p+h-(1-\beta)t\bigr]$, so
$\rho_c>0$ iff $r'<\bar{r}'$ and $\rho_c<0$ iff $r'>\bar{r}'$. By
Result~\ref{res:incidence-ratio}, $\Lambda$ has the same sign as $\rho_c$, so
$\Lambda<0\iff \rho_c<0\iff r'>\bar{r}'$. When $r'<\bar{r}'$, we have
$\rho_c>0$ and $\Lambda>0$, so $\Lambda-1=r'\rho_c\bigl[p+h-(1-\beta)t\bigr]$
is negative iff $r'<0$ and positive iff $0<r'<\bar{r}'$. Since $r>0$ and
$\theta>0$, the handling-cost and tax comparisons follow immediately.
\end{proof}

For the tax, platform revenue is part of social surplus. The platform
receives $t$ on a kept sale and $\beta t$ on a return, so revenue is
$G=Qt\theta$. Social surplus is $V\equiv W+G$, and $dW/dt=-Q\theta\Lambda$.
\begin{align}
  \frac{dV}{dt}
  &=
  -Q\theta\Lambda+\frac{d}{dt}(Qt\theta)
  \notag\\
  &=
  -Q\theta\Lambda+Q\theta+t\,\theta\rho_c\frac{d(Q\theta)}{dp}
  \notag\\
  &=
  -Q\theta\Lambda+Q\theta
  +t\theta\rho_c\Bigl(
    D'(p)\,\theta+Q\frac{d\theta}{dp}
  \Bigr)
  \notag\\
  &=
  -Q\theta\Lambda+Q\theta
  +t\theta\rho_c\bigl[D'(p)\,\theta-Q(1-\beta)r'(p)\bigr].
  \label{eq:dV}
\end{align}
$Q\theta$ is the expected tax on existing sales, paid by sellers and received by the platform. $(1-\beta)t$ is the tax refunded on a return, recovered by the seller and lost by the platform. Both cancel in \eqref{eq:dV}.
\begin{equation}
  \frac{dV}{dt}
  =
  D'(p)\,t\theta^{2}\rho_c
  -Q\theta\,r'(p)\rho_c(p+h).
  \label{eq:dV-simple}
\end{equation}
Without returns, $r'(p)=0$ and $\theta=1$, so \eqref{eq:dV-simple} reduces to $t\,dQ/dt$. The marginal buyer has zero surplus and the seller's receipt equals marginal cost, so each excluded unit creates marginal social surplus $t$. For $t>0$, the term is positive whenever total sales expand, and at $t=0$ it is zero. Holding purchases fixed, the price and the tax are internal transfers, so that part contributes nothing.

With returns, \eqref{eq:dV-simple} gives the ex-ante and ex-post decomposition of $dV/dt$. The first term is the ex-ante term, which holds the aggregate return rate fixed at $r(p)$. The marginal buyer has zero surplus, so a lost purchase leaves consumer surplus unchanged. At return rate $r(p)$, the seller's expected payoff on that unit is $\mu$, which equals marginal cost, so seller profit is also unchanged. The loss that remains is the platform's expected tax on the excluded sale. A kept sale pays $t$ and a returned sale pays $\beta t$, so the tax is $t\theta$. The tax passes through by $\theta\rho_c$. The excluded quantity is $dQ/dt=D'(p)\,\theta\rho_c$, and the ex-ante term is $t\theta\,dQ/dt$, which is positive whenever $t>0$ and total sales expand (and zero at $t=0$). The second term is the ex-post term from the change in the aggregate return rate. In joint surplus each additional return costs $p+h-(1-\beta)t$. The refunded tax is an internal transfer, so the social cost per return is $p+h$.

Combining the ex-ante term $D'(p)\,t\theta^{2}\rho_c$ with the three regimes
of the ex-post term in Result~\ref{res:welfare-bias} signs $dV/dt$. When
$r'(p)>\bar{r}'$, pass-through is negative, so the price and return rate fall,
sales expand, and both the ex-post term and (for $t>0$) the ex-ante term are
gains. When $0<r'(p)<\bar{r}'$, pass-through is positive, so the return rate
rises and sales contract, and the ex-post loss is reinforced for $t>0$ by an
ex-ante loss. When $r'(p)<0$, pass-through is positive and sales contract, so
the ex-post screening gain yields $dV/dt>0$ at $t=0$ and trades off against
the ex-ante loss when $t>0$.

\begin{result}[Tax and social surplus]
\label{res:tax-welfare}
For $t\geq 0$, \eqref{eq:dV-simple} can be written as
\begin{equation}
  \frac{dV}{dt}
  =
  \theta\rho_c\bigl[-Qr'(p)(p+h)+D'(p)\,t\theta\bigr].
  \label{eq:dV-sign}
\end{equation}
By Result~\ref{res:welfare-bias},
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(i)] if $r'(p)>\bar{r}'$, then $dV/dt>0$ (a higher tax raises
social surplus);
\item[(ii)] if $0<r'(p)<\bar{r}'$, then $dV/dt<0$ (a higher tax lowers
social surplus);
\item[(iii)] if $r'(p)<0$, $dV/dt$ has the sign of
$-Qr'(p)(p+h)+D'(p)\,t\theta$, which is positive at $t=0$ and trades off the
ex-post gain against the ex-ante loss for $t>0$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{result}
\begin{proof}
\eqref{eq:dV-simple} is $\theta\rho_c$ times the bracket in \eqref{eq:dV-sign}. Since $\theta,Q,p+h>0$, $t\geq 0$, and $D'(p)<0$, the sign of $\rho_c$ in Result~\ref{res:welfare-bias} gives the three cases. In~(i) $r'(p)>0$ and $\rho_c<0$, so $-Q\theta\,r'(p)\rho_c(p+h)>0$ and $D'(p)\,t\theta^{2}\rho_c\geq 0$. In~(ii) $r'(p)>0$ and $\rho_c>0$, so $-Q\theta\,r'(p)\rho_c(p+h)<0$ and $D'(p)\,t\theta^{2}\rho_c\leq 0$. In~(iii) $r'(p)<0$ and $\rho_c>0$, so $-Q\theta\,r'(p)\rho_c(p+h)>0$ while $D'(p)\,t\theta^{2}\rho_c\leq 0$, leaving the sign of $-Qr'(p)(p+h)+D'(p)\,t\theta$.
\end{proof}

When $t>0$ and $r>0$, raising the retained tax share $\beta$ lowers effective
marginal revenue on returns and shifts supply inward like a tax increase,
while the extra tax kept on existing returns is a transfer to the platform.
Both pieces scale by $rt/\theta$, which is why $\beta$ moves social surplus in the same direction as the tax.

\begin{corollary}[Retained tax share]
\label{res:beta-welfare}
Suppose $t>0$ and $r(p)>0$. Then
\begin{equation}
  \frac{dV}{d\beta}
  =
  \frac{rt}{\theta}\,\frac{dV}{dt},
  \label{eq:dV-dbeta}
\end{equation}
so $\beta$ moves social surplus in the same direction as the tax.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
By \eqref{eq:mu}, $\mu_\beta=(rt/\theta)\mu_t$ and $S_\beta=S_t=0$, so \eqref{eq:pass-through} gives $\rho_\beta=(rt/\theta)\rho_t$. Joint surplus depends on $(t,\beta)$ only through $(p,\mu)$, so $dW/d\beta=(rt/\theta)\,dW/dt$. For $G=Qt\theta$, the direct effect at a fixed price is $Qrt$ for $\beta$ and $Q\theta$ for $t$, and $Qrt=(rt/\theta)\,Q\theta$. The price effect scales by the same factor. Hence $dG/d\beta=(rt/\theta)\,dG/dt$, and adding the two derivatives gives \eqref{eq:dV-dbeta}.
\end{proof}

The ex-ante and ex-post decomposition in \eqref{eq:dV-simple} holds or varies
the aggregate return rate $r(p)$, whereas consumers adjust along two distinct
margins, the purchase margin (who buys at $v_i=p$) and the return margin
(whether inframarginal buyers keep or return the good). Because
$r'(p)=r'_b(p)+r'_s(p)$ in \eqref{eq:r-prime-decomp}, the ex-post term in
\eqref{eq:dV-simple} mixes the return-margin response $r'_b(p)$ with the
selection piece $r'_s(p)$ from the purchase margin. Using $\rho_t=\theta\rho_c$,
$Q=D(p)$, and $dQ/dt=D'(p)\rho_t=-f(p)\rho_t$, substituting
\eqref{eq:r-prime-decomp} into \eqref{eq:dV-simple} and grouping the selection
piece with the first term separates the two margins,
\begin{align}
  \frac{dV}{dt}
  &=
  t\theta\,\frac{dQ}{dt}
  -Q(p+h)\bigl[r'_s(p)+r'_b(p)\bigr]\rho_t
  \notag\\
  &=
  t\theta\,\frac{dQ}{dt}
  -D(p)(p+h)\,\frac{f(p)}{D(p)}\bigl(r(p)-\phi(p,p)\bigr)\rho_t
  -Q(p+h)\,r'_b(p)\,\rho_t
  \notag\\
  &=
  \Bigl[
    t\theta-\bigl(\phi(p,p)-r(p)\bigr)(p+h)
  \Bigr]\frac{dQ}{dt}
  -Q(p+h)\,r'_b(p)\,\rho_t.
  \label{eq:dV-margins}
\end{align}

The first term is the purchase margin, where the bracket is the marginal
social surplus of the sale at $v_i=p$, equal to marginal social benefit minus
marginal social cost. Holding inframarginal return behavior fixed, there is
underprovision on the purchase margin when this bracket is positive and
overprovision when it is negative. Because the marginal buyer has zero
consumer surplus, expected match value when kept minus expected return hassle
equals expected payment $\bigl(1-\phi(p,p)\bigr)p$, which is the marginal
social benefit. Marginal social cost is marginal production cost plus
expected handling $\phi(p,p)h$. Competitive sellers set marginal production
cost equal to effective marginal revenue $\mu$ at the average return rate
$r(p)$, so marginal social benefit minus marginal social cost is
\begin{align*}
  \bigl(1-\phi(p,p)\bigr)p-\bigl[\mu+\phi(p,p)h\bigr]
  &=
  \bigl(1-\phi(p,p)\bigr)p
  -\bigl[\bigl(1-r(p)\bigr)(p-t)-r(p)(\beta t+h)+\phi(p,p)h\bigr]
  \\
  &=
  t\theta-\bigl(\phi(p,p)-r(p)\bigr)(p+h).
\end{align*}

Without selection ($r'_s(p)=0$, so $\phi(p,p)=r(p)$), marginal social surplus
at $v_i=p$ equals the tax wedge $t\theta$, giving underprovision for any $t>0$
and no distortion at $t=0$. With selection ($r'_s(p)\neq 0$), sellers cannot
observe buyer types and choose quantity from effective marginal revenue at the
average return rate $r(p)$ rather than the marginal return rate $\phi(p,p)$,
which shifts marginal social surplus by $-\bigl(\phi(p,p)-r(p)\bigr)(p+h)$.
When $r'_s(p)<0$ ($\phi(p,p)>r(p)$), low-return inframarginal buyers
cross-subsidize high-return marginal buyers through the single market price.
Because each extra return costs society $p+h$, this cross-subsidy causes
overprovision whenever $\bigl(\phi(p,p)-r(p)\bigr)(p+h)>t\theta$, and
therefore whenever $r'_s(p)<0$ at $t=0$. Conversely, when $r'_s(p)>0$
($\phi(p,p)<r(p)$), marginal buyers return less often than average, so
selection raises marginal social surplus and yields underprovision for all
$t\geq 0$. Finally, the second term in \eqref{eq:dV-margins},
$-Q(p+h)\,r'_b(p)\,\rho_t$, is the return margin and keeps only the behavioral
response $r'_b(p)$ of inframarginal buyers.
Result~\ref{res:efficiency-margins} summarizes both margins.

\begin{result}[Purchase and return margins]
\label{res:efficiency-margins}
Equation \eqref{eq:dV-margins} separates the purchase and return margins, where
$dQ/dt=D'(p)\rho_t$ has the opposite sign of $\rho_t$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(i)] On the purchase margin, there is overprovision if and only if
$\bigl(\phi(p,p)-r(p)\bigr)(p+h)>t\theta$, and underprovision when the
inequality is reversed. Under overprovision, a higher tax raises social surplus
on the purchase margin if $\rho_t>0$ ($dQ/dt<0$) and lowers it if $\rho_t<0$
($dQ/dt>0$), with the opposite signs under underprovision. Without selection
($r'_s(p)=0$), any $t>0$ yields underprovision. At $t=0$, selection yields
overprovision if and only if $r'_s(p)<0$ ($\phi(p,p)>r(p)$) and underprovision
if and only if $r'_s(p)>0$ ($\phi(p,p)<r(p)$).
\item[(ii)] On the return margin, when $r'_b(p)>0$, a higher tax lowers
social surplus on the return margin if $\rho_t>0$ and raises it if $\rho_t<0$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{result}

This departs sharply from the competitive benchmark without returns, where an
untaxed market is efficient and a tax causes only underprovision on the
purchase margin. With returns and a single market price, both overprovision
($r'_s(p)<0$) and underprovision ($r'_s(p)>0$) can arise even without a tax,
governed by the sign of the selection channel. Whenever negative selection
causes overprovision and pass-through is positive ($\rho_t>0$, so $dQ/dt<0$), a
higher tax is corrective on the purchase margin because the higher list price
deters inefficient marginal purchases. Starting from $t=0$,
\eqref{eq:dV-margins} reduces to
$dV/dt=-Q(p+h)\bigl[r'_s(p)+r'_b(p)\bigr]\rho_t$. Under $\rho_t>0$ with
$r'_s(p)<0$ and $r'_b(p)>0$, the higher price improves the purchase margin
while worsening the return margin, so a small tax raises total social surplus
if and only if selection dominates behavior ($r'(p)<0$, as in
Result~\ref{res:tax-welfare}(iii)).

\section{Global Incidence}
\label{sec:global-incidence}
%=============================================================================

Each row of Table~\ref{tab:incidence_components} is the local derivative at
the current equilibrium, using the current $\mu_p$ and $\rho_c$. A finite
change in production cost, handling cost, or the tax integrates that row,
with the row re-evaluated at the current equilibrium.

Along such a path, the global incidence ratio
$I_{x_0}^{x_1}\equiv \Delta CS/\Delta PS$, when $\Delta PS\neq 0$, is the
weighted average of the local ratio implied by $dCS/dx = I(x)\,dPS/dx$,
\begin{equation}
I_{x_0}^{x_1} = \int_{x_0}^{x_1} I(x)\,\omega(x)\,dx,
\label{eq:global-incidence-final}
\end{equation}
where $\omega(x)\equiv (dPS/dx)/\Delta PS$.

The global ratio can be negative, even though $I(x)$ is positive at every
point on the path. If $dPS/dx$ changes sign along the path, the weights
$\omega(x)$ take both signs, and the weighted average of positive local
ratios can be negative. A finite change therefore need not preserve the
sign of local incidence.
