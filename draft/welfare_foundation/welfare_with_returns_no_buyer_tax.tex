\section{Introduction}
%=============================================================================

Who bears a tax, and how much of it passes through into the price, is the
question of tax incidence \citep{weyl2013pass}. A sales tax and a platform's
referral fee are examples. The pass-through and incidence of such taxes are
well studied when purchases are final. Online purchases, however,
are often returned. Retailers expect about one in five online sales to be
returned.\footnote{National Retail
  Federation and Happy Returns, \emph{2025 Retail Returns Landscape}, October
  15, 2025, \url{https://nrf.com/research/2025-retail-returns-landscape}.
  The report estimates that 19.3 percent of online sales will be returned in
  2025.} The seller receives the price only when the buyer keeps the good.
Every unit sold therefore bears not only its own tax but also the costs of
returned units, such as the tax not fully recovered and the return handling
cost. This extra cost can make pass-through stronger. The price, however, also affects the return rate. A higher price
can raise returns, because a higher refund makes returning more attractive, or
lower them, when it screens out lower-valued buyers, who return more than the
average buyer. The return rate, the seller's profit margin, and the quantity
supplied can therefore move in either direction with the price. So it is not clear whether
tax pass-through is stronger or weaker with returns, or even negative. The question applies to supply-side shocks
more generally, such as the tax, production
cost, which is incurred on every sale, and return handling cost, which is
incurred only when the buyer returns the product.

In practice, the tax on a sale also depends on whether the product is kept
or returned. When a purchase is returned, the sales tax is refunded in full. A platform's
referral fee is refunded only in part. Amazon deducts the fee from each sale,
15 percent of the sale price in many categories, and keeps up to 20 percent of
it on a return.\footnote{Amazon,
  \emph{Selling on Amazon pricing},
  \url{https://sell.amazon.com/pricing}, and \emph{North America selling fees},
  \url{https://sell.amazon.com.sg/north-america/pricing}. Amazon calls the
  amount it keeps the refund administration fee, the lesser of five dollars or
  20 percent of the referral fee.} Whether the seller should recover the tax
in full on a return, or the platform should be allowed to keep part of it, is
an open question. Platforms defend keeping part of the fee as covering the
cost of handling returns, yet that part is kept whenever a paid order is
refunded, even if the product never shipped. This paper studies the pass-through, incidence, and welfare effects of
the tax in a competitive market, including how much to tax a kept sale and a return. Each buyer
decides whether to buy and, after learning the fit, whether to keep the good,
and each seller decides how much to supply. These supply-side
shocks reach the buyer's purchase and return decisions only through the price.
They reach the seller directly, as part of the cost of each unit sold, and so
shift supply.

A tax creates a wedge between what the buyer will pay and what the good costs
the seller. Without returns, the wedge leads to underprovision on the purchase
margin, because some beneficial trades do not happen. Returns add a second
margin, the decision to keep the good, and a higher price can raise returns,
because buyers who remain need a better fit to keep the good, or lower them,
when the consumers it screens out return more than the average buyer. It is
therefore not clear whether the tax is
still costly to society, as it is without returns, or in which direction the
no-return results are biased when returns are ignored.

The paper proceeds in four steps, each for a small change in the tax. First,
how does the tax pass through into the price, and how do buyers and sellers
divide the change in their own surplus? Production cost and return handling
cost serve as benchmarks. A partly refunded tax lies between the two. Second,
counting the revenue from the tax as part of social surplus, does any loss to
society come from the decision to buy or from the decision to keep the good,
and in which direction does that decision go wrong? Third, what is missed by
ignoring returns and using the no-return results for this tax? Fourth, if a
platform keeps a larger share of the tax on a return, does society gain or
lose? The answers depend only on the return rate and on how demand, supply,
and returns respond to the price.

%=============================================================================
\section{Model}
%=============================================================================

We study the pass-through of a tax charged by the platform and of a
cost increase, such as a rise in return handling cost or in production
cost, in a homogeneous-good market under perfect competition with
product returns. The product sells at list price $p$. Consumers indexed by $i$ differ in
return hassle $\kappa_i$, the disutility of sending the good back, and in
the distribution $G_i$ of post-purchase match quality $u$. After buying,
type $i$ draws $u\sim G_i$. In the baseline model, the refund is restricted
to equal the list price, so the consumer's utility is $u-p$ when he keeps
the product and $-\kappa_i$ when he returns it.

Individual consumer surplus from buying at price $p$ is
\[
  cs_i(p)=\mathbb{E}_{u\sim G_i}\bigl[\max(u-p,\,-\kappa_i)\bigr].
\]
Consumer $i$'s purchase decision can be summarized by willingness to pay
$v_i$, defined as the price solving $cs_i(v_i)=0$. Type $i$ buys if and
only if $p\leq v_i$. Across types, $v_i$ has distribution $F$ with density
$f$, so gross demand is $D(p)=1-F(p)$.

Consumer $i$ returns the item with probability
$\phi_i(p)=\Pr(u<p-\kappa_i)$.
Averaging it over consumers with the same willingness to pay $v$ gives
$\phi(v,p)\equiv\mathbb{E}\bigl[\phi_i(p)\bigm|v_i=v\bigr]$.
The aggregate return rate $r(p)$ is derived by further taking the
average over consumers who make a purchase at $p$, equal to
\[
  \frac{1}{D(p)}\int_p^\infty \phi(v,p)\,dF(v).
\]
The return rate depends on how consumers purchase and on how each
purchaser returns. The price shapes the return rate through both.
Therefore, the effect of the price can be decomposed into a behavioral
channel and a selection channel,
\[
  r'(p)
  =
  \underbrace{
    \frac{1}{D(p)}\int_p^\infty \frac{\partial \phi(v,p)}{\partial p}\,dF(v)
  }_{\text{Behavioral channel}}
  +
  \underbrace{
    \frac{f(p)}{D(p)}\bigl(r(p)-\phi(p,p)\bigr)
  }_{\text{Selection channel}},
\]
The behavioral channel is the change in return probability holding the
purchase cutoff fixed. A higher price lowers the utility of keeping, so
purchasers return more often. The selection channel captures the change in
who buys. Its sign is undetermined without additional assumptions. In
general, this channel is non-positive because higher willingness to pay
lowers the return probability, so buyers at the margin return more often
than the average buyer and $\phi(p,p)\geq r(p)$.\footnote{As when
$u=v_i+\varepsilon$, with $\varepsilon$ independent of $v_i$ and return
hassle independent of $v_i$.} The two channels then move $r'(p)$ in opposite
directions. Below, $r'(p)$ is left unsigned.

Producer $j$ takes the list price $p$ and the
return rate $r$ as given and chooses quantity $q_j$ at cost $C_j(q_j)$, with
strictly increasing marginal cost. The platform charges an ad valorem
tax at rate $\tau$, so the seller keeps $(1-\tau)p$ on a kept unit. On a returned unit the seller refunds the full
price, so the buyer pays nothing. The seller pays net handling $h$ on
the good, which is gross handling cost minus scrap value, and a share $\beta\in[0,1]$ of the tax is retained by
the platform on each returned unit. The seller therefore
loses $\beta\tau p + h$ on that returned unit.\footnote{Amazon's refund
  administration fee is this rule for a referral fee.  On a return Amazon
  keeps the lesser of \$5 or 20 percent of the referral fee, and gives
  the rest back to the seller.  When the \$5 cap does not bind,
  $\beta=0.2$.}  We maintain $p+h>0$, which holds whenever scrap value
does not exceed list price. With return rate $r(p)$, the seller's
expected payoff per unit is the effective marginal revenue
\begin{equation}
  \mu(p,h,\tau,\beta)
  =
  \bigl(1-r(p)\bigr)(1-\tau)p - r(p)(\beta\tau p + h).
  \label{eq:mu}
\end{equation}

%=============================================================================
\subsection{Welfare measure}
%=============================================================================

Consumer surplus is the sum of individual consumer surplus over buyers who
purchase. A tax or a cost shock affects consumer surplus only through
the list price. A higher price removes the marginal buyer, who had zero
surplus, so the lost purchase does not reduce consumer surplus. For an
inframarginal buyer, who still purchases, the utility of keeping falls by
the price increase. The utility of returning does not change, because the
higher price raises the payment and the refund by the same amount. The
return probability rises, but the buyer who switches from keeping to
returning was indifferent, so the switch does not reduce consumer
surplus. The remaining loss is one unit of utility on each unit that is
kept. There are $D(p)(1-r(p))$ such units, so
\begin{equation}
  CS'(p)=-D(p)\bigl(1-r(p)\bigr).
\end{equation}

Producer surplus is the sum of firm profits
$\mu q_j-C_j(q_j)$. A tax or a cost shock affects producer surplus
only through the effective marginal revenue, and by the envelope theorem a
one-dollar rise in the effective marginal revenue raises a firm's profit by the
quantity it chooses. Aggregating quantity supplied over firms gives
\begin{equation}
  \frac{dPS}{d\mu}=S(\mu),
\end{equation}
where $S(\mu)$ is market supply.

%=============================================================================
\section{Pass-through}
%=============================================================================

We study pass-through of a production-cost increase, a handling-cost
increase, and the platform's ad valorem tax, and denote these pass-throughs
by the subscripts $c$, $h$, and $\tau$. For the production-cost increase, we
consider a parallel increase in marginal production cost, so marginal
production cost is $C_j'(q_j)+c$. These three shocks shift supply but not
demand, because consumers' purchase and return decisions respond only to
the list price $p$.

Pass-through $\rho_x\equiv dp/dx$, for $x\in\{c,h,\tau\}$, is determined by the
market clearing condition. Its general form is
\begin{equation}
  m(p,x)\equiv D(p)-S\bigl(\mu(p,x),x\bigr).
\end{equation}
Market clearing requires $m(p,x)=0$. All three pass-throughs are
evaluated at $\tau=0$. Differentiating $m=0$ gives
\begin{equation}
  \rho_x
  =
  \frac{-S'(\mu)\,\mu_{x}-S_x}{S'(\mu)\,\mu_{p}-D'(p)}.
  \label{eq:pass-through}
\end{equation}
Here $\mu_{p}$ and $\mu_{x}$ are partial derivatives of
$\mu(p,x)$, and $S_x$ is the direct shift of supply at a fixed
effective marginal revenue. The production-cost shock does not enter $\mu$, so
$\mu_{c}=0$, yet a unit increase in $c$ shifts supply as if $\mu$
fell by one unit, so $S_c=-S'(\mu)$. The handling cost and the tax have no
direct effect on supply at a fixed effective marginal revenue, so $S_h=S_{\tau}=0$.
The handling cost reduces the effective marginal revenue when a consumer returns the
product, so $\mu_{h}=-r(p)$. The tax reduces the seller's effective marginal revenue
on each purchase, but a return restores the share $1-\beta$ of that tax, so
$\mu_{\tau}=-\theta$ where
$\theta\equiv p\bigl[1-(1-\beta)r(p)\bigr]$. Substituting these into
\eqref{eq:pass-through} gives the three pass-throughs,
\begin{equation}
  \rho_c
  = \frac{S'(\mu)}{S'(\mu)\,\mu_{p}-D'(p)},
  \qquad
  \rho_h = r(p)\,\rho_c,
  \qquad
  \rho_{\tau} = \theta\,\rho_c,
  \label{eq:rho-links}
\end{equation}
In the classical model without returns, a unit tax rate and a unit
production-cost increase create the same per-dollar wedge, so
$\rho_{\tau}=p\,\rho_c$ differs from $\rho_c$ only by the ad valorem
scaling $p$. With returns, each shock shifts the supply curve to the left
by $S'(\mu)$ times a scale factor. The factor is $1$ for production
cost, $r$ for handling cost, and $\theta$ for the tax.
Since supply and demand are locally linear, pass-through depends only
on how far the supply curve shifts. That shift is proportional to the
factor, so each pass-through is $\rho_c$ scaled by its factor. Since $\rho_h$ and $\rho_{\tau}$ are scalings of $\rho_c$, below we
focus on $\rho_c$. The signs of $\rho_h$ and $\rho_{\tau}$ are the same.

These pass-throughs take a more transparent form in price
elasticities, which also exposes the role of returns. Let
$\mu_p^0\equiv\mu_{p}\big|_{\tau=0}$ be the slope of effective
marginal revenue with respect to price at zero tax. The demand and supply elasticities are
\[
\varepsilon_D(p)\equiv\frac{p D'(p)}{D(p)}<0,
\qquad
\varepsilon_S(p)\equiv S'(\mu)\,\mu_p^0\,\frac{p}{S(\mu)}.
\]
The supply elasticity $\varepsilon_S$ has the sign of $\mu_p^0$. With no
returns, $\mu_p^0=1$ and the supply elasticity is positive. With returns,
the supply elasticity can be negative if the return rate is an
increasing function of price. When the behavioral channel dominates, a
higher price raises the return rate, reduces the effective marginal revenue, and
lowers quantity supplied.

Multiplying numerator and denominator of $\rho_c$ by $p/S(\mu)$
gives the elasticity form,
\begin{equation}
  \rho_c
  =
  \frac{\varepsilon_S(p)/\mu_p^0}
       {-\varepsilon_D(p)+\varepsilon_S(p)}.
  \label{eq:rho_c_elasticity_final}
\end{equation}
The pass-through $\rho_c$ is the ratio of the excess-demand gap a
cost increase opens to the rate at which a price change closes it. A
production-cost increase reduces supply by $S'(\mu)$ and opens excess
demand of that size. An increase in the list price closes that gap at
rate $S'(\mu)\,\mu_p^0-D'(p)$. Compared to the classical no-return
formula, which sets $\mu_p^0=1$, the returns formula scales the
pass-through by $1/\mu_p^0$. When $\mu_p^0$ is above unity, one dollar
of list price restores more than one dollar of effective marginal revenue, so a
smaller price movement closes the gap. When $0<\mu_p^0<1$, the return
rate rises in price and one dollar of list price restores less than
one dollar of effective marginal revenue, so the price must move more and
pass-through is larger than the classical value. When $\mu_p^0$ is
negative, the effective marginal revenue decreases in the list price, and the
sign of pass-through depends on the magnitudes of the elasticities. In
particular, negative pass-through can happen when supply is more
elastic than demand in absolute value, so lowering the price raises
supply by more than it raises demand and clears the excess demand.
This is in sharp contrast to the model without returns, where
pass-through is always positive. The following two results summarize
the findings.
\begin{result}[Negative pass-through]
\label{res:negative-pt}
  We have $\rho_c<0$ if and only if $\varepsilon_S<0$ ($\mu_p^0<0$) and
  $|\varepsilon_S|>-\varepsilon_D$.
\end{result}
\begin{result}
\label{res:pt-bias-er}
  Relative to the correct returns-adjusted $\rho_c$, the classical
  no-return pass-through formula overestimates pass-through when
  $\mu_p^0>1$, agrees with it when $\mu_p^0=1$, and underestimates it
  when $0\leq\mu_p^0<1$.
\end{result}

Result~\ref{res:pt-bias-er} can be linked to the handling
cost and which channel of $r'(p)$ dominates.
The slope of effective marginal revenue is likely to be above one when the selection
channel dominates and the handling cost is large, and to fall
below one when the behavioral channel dominates and the handling
cost is large. Differentiating effective marginal revenue at $\tau=0$
gives $\mu_p^0=1-r(p)-(p+h)\,r'(p)$. A strong selection channel has $r'(p)<0$, so a higher price
lowers the return rate and saves handling cost, pushing
$\mu_p^0$ above one, whereas a strong behavioral channel has
$r'(p)>0$, so a higher price raises the return rate and adds
handling cost, pushing $\mu_p^0$ below one. A larger handling cost
amplifies both effects.

%=============================================================================
\section{Local incidence}
%=============================================================================

Having established how prices respond to different shocks, we turn to
local incidence, defined as the ratio of the change in consumer
surplus to the change in producer surplus for a small shock $x$. Before a
discussion of the incidence, it is useful to visualize consumer
surplus and producer surplus in a graph.

Figure~\ref{fig:cs_ps_mu_sign} shows demand, the inverse supply curve,
consumer surplus, producer surplus, and how the inverse supply curve
moves after a production-cost increase. The inverse supply curve gives
the price required to supply a given quantity. Consumer surplus is the
area between gross demand $D(p)$ and returns $D(p)r(p)$ above $p$.
Producer surplus is the area to the left of the supply curve and below
$p$, between zero and $p$. For a unit production-cost increase, quantity
supplied decreases at a fixed price, and the reduction is the same as a
one-unit fall in the effective marginal revenue. To supply the same quantity, the
price must adjust by $1/\mu_p^0$, so that the effective marginal revenue rises by
exactly one unit to counteract the production-cost increase.

The left panel shows the case where the selection channel of the return rate
is stronger than the behavioral channel, so that $r(p)$ falls with $p$. This
can be seen from the graph as returns $D(p)r(p)$ taking a smaller share of
gross demand $D(p)$ at a higher $p$. A higher $p$ screens out consumers with a high return probability,
so the effective marginal revenue improves and the seller supplies more. A positive
slope means $\mu_p^0>0$. A unit production-cost increase shifts the
inverse supply curve up by $1/\mu_p^0$ at a given quantity, and the
equilibrium price rises.

The right panel shows the case where the behavioral channel of the return
rate is stronger than the selection channel, so that $r(p)$ rises with $p$.
This can be seen from the graph as returns $D(p)r(p)$ taking a larger share
of gross demand $D(p)$ at a higher $p$. A higher $p$ raises the return
probability for purchasers, which lowers the effective marginal revenue and reduces
the quantity the seller supplies. The inverse supply curve is hump-shaped. It is not profitable to
supply at a high $p$, because the return rate is so high that almost any
sale leads to a return. It is also not profitable to supply at a low $p$,
because the low profit margin is not enough to cover the production cost.
On the downward-sloping region, $\mu_p^0<0$. A unit production-cost
increase shifts the inverse supply curve down by $1/|\mu_p^0|$ at a given
quantity, and the equilibrium price falls.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{../../figuretable/output/cs_ps_mu_sign.png}
\caption{Consumer surplus, producer surplus, and supply after a
production-cost increase.}
\label{fig:cs_ps_mu_sign}
\end{figure}

One observation is that the incidence is the same for the three shocks.
Intuitively, at a fixed price each shock reduces quantity supplied as if the
effective marginal revenue had fallen. The three falls differ only in magnitude. Supply and demand are locally
linear, so pass-through and the quantity change depend only on that magnitude.
The changes in consumer surplus and producer surplus are both locally linear
in how far the supply curve shifts, and that magnitude cancels in the ratio.
Mathematically, let $Q\equiv D(p)$ and
$r\equiv r(p)$ denote the equilibrium gross quantity and the return
rate. A shock $x$ affects consumer surplus only through the price, so
$\frac{dCS}{dx}=-Q(1-r)\rho_x$. This change scales with $\rho_x$ and hence
with the local supply shift.
Producer surplus, written as a function of $x$, is
\[
  PS(x)=\int_0^{\mu(p(x),x)} S(z,x)\,dz.
\]
A shock $x$ affects producer surplus through pass-through, through the direct
effect on the effective marginal revenue, holding the price fixed, and through a shift
of the supply curve, holding the price and effective marginal revenue fixed.
Differentiating in $x$ and using
$S\bigl(\mu(p(x),x),x\bigr)=Q$ gives
$dPS/dx=Q\,d\mu(p(x),x)/dx+\Sigma_x$, where
$\Sigma_x\equiv\int_0^{\mu(p(x),x)}(\partial S(z,x)/\partial x)\,dz$.
Because $d\mu(p(x),x)/dx=\mu_p^0\rho_x+\mu_x$,
\begin{equation}
  \frac{dPS}{dx} = Q\mu_p^0\rho_x + Q\mu_x + \Sigma_x.
  \label{eq:PSx_incidence}
\end{equation}
Table~\ref{tab:incidence_components} shows that the joint effect of these three
channels is also proportional to pass-through and to the degree of the local
supply shift.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{lccccccc}
\hline
Shock $x$ & $\rho_x$ & $\mu_x$ & $\Sigma_x$ & $dCS/dx$ & $dPS/dx$ & $I_x$ \\
\hline
Production cost $c$ & $\rho_c$ & $0$ & $-Q$ & $-Q(1-r)\rho_c$ & $Q(\mu_p^0\rho_c - 1)$ & $\displaystyle \frac{(1-r)\rho_c}{1-\mu_p^0\rho_c}$ \\

\hline
\end{tabular}
\caption{Local incidence for each shock. The tax column is evaluated at a zero rate.}
\label{tab:incidence_components}
\end{table}

The local incidence ratio is therefore the same for the three shocks. The next
result formalizes this and shows that the common ratio is positive.

\begin{result}
\label{res:incidence-ratio}
For production cost, handling cost, and the tax, the local incidence
ratio is identical and positive.
\begin{equation}
  I_x = \frac{(1-r)\rho_c}{1-\mu_p^0 \rho_c}>0.
  \label{eq:incidence_result}
\end{equation}
\end{result}
\begin{proof}
Since $1-r>0$, the ratio is positive if $\rho_c$ has the same sign as
$1-\mu_p^0\rho_c$. Multiplying the pass-through formula
\eqref{eq:rho_c_elasticity_final} by $\mu_p^0$ gives
$\mu_p^0\rho_c=\varepsilon_S/(\varepsilon_S-\varepsilon_D)$.
This exceeds one if and only if $\varepsilon_S<0$ and $|\varepsilon_S|>-\varepsilon_D$.
Result~\ref{res:negative-pt} states that this is the necessary and sufficient
condition for $\rho_c<0$.
\end{proof}

For a unit increase in production cost, the numerator $(1-r)\rho_c$ is the
per-unit consumer loss on kept units. The denominator $1-\mu_p^0\rho_c$ is
the per-unit producer loss. Before the price adjusts, the direct effect
through the supply curve shift lowers producer surplus by $Q$. The indirect price
pass-through effect moves the effective marginal revenue by $\mu_p^0\rho_c$ per unit,
and the envelope $dPS/d\mu=Q$ restores producer surplus by $Q\mu_p^0\rho_c$.
Together, the net change in producer surplus is $Q(\mu_p^0\rho_c-1)$.

The incidence is positive even when pass-through is negative because the
recovery from the indirect price pass-through effect more than offsets the
direct loss from the supply curve shift. With negative pass-through, the
new equilibrium price is lower and quantity demanded is higher. Market clearing requires the new equilibrium
quantity supplied to exceed the original quantity. The price channel must
therefore raise quantity supplied by more than the initial decrease caused
by the inward shift of the supply curve. This implies that the price
channel raises the effective marginal revenue by more than one unit, so the
denominator is negative when pass-through is negative. The reasoning in
the opposite direction holds for positive pass-through.

The result has a striking implication that a production cost increase can be
Pareto-improving. The uniformly positive incidence ratio means consumer and
producer surplus always move together, regardless of the sign of
pass-through. When pass-through is negative, raising production cost,
handling cost, or the tax lowers the price, reduces return rates, and saves
enough handling costs to more than offset the direct resource drain. Both
consumers and producers benefit. When pass-through is positive, all three
shocks reduce both surpluses, as in the classical model, where $\mu_p^0=1$
and $\rho_c>0$.

%=============================================================================
\subsection{First-order effect on joint surplus and ex-ante ex-post decomposition}
\label{sec:private-surplus}
%=============================================================================

Joint surplus is $W\equiv CS+PS$. A unit increase in production cost
changes it by the sum of the consumer and producer changes in
Table~\ref{tab:incidence_components},
\begin{equation}
  \frac{dW}{dc}
  =
  -Q(1-r)\rho_c+Q(\mu_p^0\rho_c-1).
  \label{eq:dW-c-raw}
\end{equation}
At a zero tax, $\mu_p^0=1-r(p)-(p+h)r'(p)$. Substituting $\mu_p^0$
into \eqref{eq:dW-c-raw} gives
\begin{equation}
  \frac{dW}{dc}=-Q\Lambda,
  \qquad
  \Lambda\equiv 1+r'(p)(p+h)\rho_c.
  \label{eq:Lambda}
\end{equation}
Pass-through of the production cost changes the price by $\rho_c$ on each
unit. Without returns, buyers lose $\rho_c$ on each of the $Q$ units sold, so
they lose $Q\rho_c$. Sellers gain $\rho_c$ on each of those units, so they
gain the same amount. These price effects cancel, so what remains is the
direct effect of the higher production cost on the equilibrium quantity
sold, a change of $-Q$.

With returns, hold fixed who buys and who returns. Buyers then lose
$\rho_c$ on each kept unit and lose nothing on a returned unit, because the
refund equals the price. There are $Q(1-r)$ kept units, so buyers lose
$Q(1-r)\rho_c$. Sellers gain the same amount on those units. These price
effects still cancel, and the direct loss $-Q$ remains.

A change in who buys or who returns does not add a further consumer loss. A
buyer who stops buying had zero surplus. The higher price lowers the value
of keeping, but the buyer who switches from keep to return was indifferent
between the two, so the switch offsets that lower value. The cost of the
switch falls on the seller, who gives up the price $p$ and pays the handling
cost $h$. The price moves the return rate by $r'(p)\rho_c$, so these
switches change joint surplus by $-Q(p+h)r'(p)\rho_c$. Added to the direct
loss $-Q$, the change is $-Q\Lambda$.

Handling cost and the tax scale this change by $r$ and by $\theta$. In
Table~\ref{tab:incidence_components}, their consumer and producer changes are
the production-cost changes multiplied by those factors. Both pieces of
$\Lambda$ therefore scale, and
\begin{equation}
  \frac{dW}{dh}=-Qr\Lambda,
  \qquad
  \frac{dW}{d\tau}=-Q\theta\Lambda.
  \label{eq:dW-shocks}
\end{equation}

Freezing the return rate separates an ex-ante rectangle on the purchase margin
from an ex-post term on the keep-or-return margin. With $r'(p)=0$, the factor
$\Lambda$ equals one. A unit production-cost increase then shifts supply up by
one dollar at every quantity. The price changes in consumer and producer
surplus cancel, and joint surplus falls by the rectangle of height one and
width $Q$. Handling cost leaves the rectangle $Qr$, and the tax leaves the
rectangle $Q\theta$. These rectangles are the ex-ante term, what the change
would be if returns did not respond to the price. The gap between the actual
change $-Q\Lambda$ and this rectangle is the ex-post term
$-Q\,r'(p)(p+h)\rho_c$, scaled by $r$ or by $\theta$ for the other two shocks.
\begin{result}[Ex-ante and ex-post decomposition]
\label{res:EA-EP}
\begin{align}
  \frac{dW}{dc}
  &=
  -Q-Q\,r'(p)(p+h)\rho_c,
  \label{eq:dW-decomp} \\
  \frac{dW}{dh}
  &=
  -Qr-Qr\,r'(p)(p+h)\rho_c, \\
  \frac{dW}{d\tau}
  &=
  -Q\theta-Q\theta\,r'(p)(p+h)\rho_c.
\end{align}
\end{result}

The ex-post term offsets, amplifies, or reverses the rectangle, and these
cases line up with the sign of pass-through.
Offsetting means $0<\Lambda<1$, amplification means $\Lambda>1$, and reversal
means $\Lambda<0$. Because $dW/dc=-Q\Lambda$, joint surplus falls when
$\Lambda$ is positive and rises when $\Lambda$ is negative.
Result~\ref{res:incidence-ratio} says that the two surpluses move together.
They fall when $\rho_c>0$ and rise when $\rho_c<0$, so $\Lambda$ has the same
sign as $\rho_c$.

When the selection channel dominates, the ex-post term offsets part of the
rectangle and cannot reverse the loss. Then $r'(p)<0$, so $\mu_p^0>0$ and
pass-through is positive. The list price rises and screens out frequent
returners, so the return rate falls. The product $r'(p)\rho_c$ is therefore
negative, and the ex-post term $-Q\,r'(p)(p+h)\rho_c$ is a gain that cuts the
loss from $Q$ to $Q\Lambda$ with $0<\Lambda<1$. The offset is larger when
$|r'(p)|$ or $h$ is larger.

When the behavioral channel dominates and pass-through is positive, the
ex-post term amplifies the rectangle. Then $r'(p)>0$ and $\rho_c>0$. The list
price rises and the return rate rises with it, so $r'(p)(p+h)\rho_c$ is
positive, $\Lambda>1$, and $-Q\,r'(p)(p+h)\rho_c$ is a further loss. The added
loss is larger when $r'(p)$ or $h$ is larger.

When pass-through is negative, the ex-post term reverses the rectangle and
joint surplus rises. Result~\ref{res:negative-pt} requires $\mu_p^0<0$ and
therefore $r'(p)>0$. The list price falls and the return rate falls with it,
so $-Q\,r'(p)(p+h)\rho_c$ is again a gain. Because $\Lambda<0$, the gain is
larger than the rectangle $Q$, and joint surplus rises by $Q|\Lambda|$. The
rise is larger when $r'(p)(p+h)|\rho_c|$ is larger.

The no-return formula keeps the rectangle and omits the ex-post term, so its
entire error is that omission. For a production-cost increase it reports a
loss of $Q$ against the true change $-Q\Lambda$, and the three regimes above
translate directly into the direction of the error.
\begin{result}[No-return joint-surplus bias]
\label{res:welfare-bias}
Consider a unit production-cost increase. Relative to the returns-adjusted
change $dW/dc=-Q\Lambda$, the ex-ante formula $dW/dc=-Q$
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(i)] overestimates the joint-surplus loss if and only if $r'(p)<0$,
in which case $0<\Lambda<1$;
\item[(ii)] underestimates the joint-surplus loss if and only if
$r'(p)>0$ and $\rho_c>0$, in which case $\Lambda>1$;
\item[(iii)] overestimates the joint-surplus loss with the wrong sign if
and only if $r'(p)>0$ and $\rho_c<0$. It reports a loss of $Q$ when joint
surplus rises by $Q|\Lambda|$.
\end{enumerate}
For a handling-cost shock the same cuts compare $-Qr\Lambda$ with $-Qr$. For
the tax they compare $-Q\theta\Lambda$ with $-Q\theta$.
\end{result}
\begin{proof}
The three cases above give the direct statements. For the converses, suppose
$0<\Lambda<1$. Then $\rho_c>0$ and $r'(p)\rho_c<0$, so $r'(p)<0$. This is
(i). Suppose $\Lambda>1$. Then $r'(p)\rho_c>0$, and $\Lambda>0$ forces
$\rho_c>0$, so $r'(p)>0$. This is (ii). Suppose $\Lambda<0$. Then
$\rho_c<0$ and therefore $r'(p)>0$. This is (iii). The tax multiplies the
true change and the benchmark by $\theta$. This factor is positive because
$\theta=p[1-(1-\beta)r]\geq p(1-r)$ and $1-r>0$. The handling-cost comparison
is unchanged when $r>0$.
\end{proof}

For the tax, adding platform revenue cancels the ex-ante rectangle and leaves
the ex-post term as the social effect. The platform receives $\tau p$ on a
kept sale and $\beta\tau p$ on a return, so revenue is $G=\tau\theta Q$.
Social surplus is $V\equiv W+G$. At a zero tax, marginal revenue equals the
rectangle $\theta Q$. Adding it to $dW/d\tau=-Q\theta\Lambda$ gives
\begin{equation}
  \frac{dV}{d\tau}
  =
  -Q\theta\Lambda+\theta Q
  =
  -Q\theta\,r'(p)(p+h)\rho_c.
  \label{eq:dV}
\end{equation}
The surviving term is $\theta$ times the ex-post term. Without returns,
$r'(p)=0$ and $\theta=p$, so an infinitesimal tax only transfers $pQ$ to the
platform and social surplus does not change. With returns, society gains when
the tax lowers the return rate and loses when the tax raises it. The gain
occurs in the offsetting and reversal cases, when selection dominates or
pass-through is negative. The loss occurs in the amplification case, when the
behavioral channel dominates and pass-through is positive.
At a zero tax, $\beta$ does not enter $\rho_c$, because the retained share
multiplies $\tau$ in the effective marginal revenue. A larger $\beta$ raises $\theta$
and scales the social effect. The sign does not change.

\section{Global Incidence}
\label{sec:global-incidence}
%=============================================================================

The tax row of Table~\ref{tab:incidence_components} is the derivative at a
zero rate. It is not the integrand for a finite change in $\tau$.

A finite production-cost or handling-cost change integrates that shock's row
in the same table, with the row re-evaluated at the current equilibrium.

Along such a path, the global incidence ratio
$I_{x_0}^{x_1}\equiv \Delta CS/\Delta PS$, when $\Delta PS\neq 0$, is the
weighted average of the local ratio implied by $dCS/dx = I(x)\,dPS/dx$,
\begin{equation}
I_{x_0}^{x_1} = \int_{x_0}^{x_1} I(x)\,\omega(x)\,dx,
\label{eq:global-incidence-final}
\end{equation}
where $\omega(x)\equiv (dPS/dx)/\Delta PS$.

The global ratio can be negative, even though $I(x)$ is positive at every
point on the path. If $dPS/dx$ changes sign along the path, the weights
$\omega(x)$ take both signs, and the weighted average of positive local
ratios can be negative. A finite production-cost or handling-cost change
therefore need not preserve the sign of local incidence.

\section{Welfare bias in primitives}
\label{app:welfare-bias-primitives}
Result~\ref{res:welfare-bias} has an equivalent statement in elasticities.
Recall $\varepsilon_r(p)\equiv p\,r'(p)/r(p)$ and
$\mu_p^0=1-r(p)-r'(p)(p+h)$. Then
$r'(p)(p+h)=\varepsilon_r(p)\,r(p)\,(p+h)/p$, so
\begin{equation}
  \mu_p^0>0
    \;\iff\;
  \varepsilon_r(p)
  <
  \frac{p}{p+h}\,\frac{1-r(p)}{r(p)},
  \label{eq:app-mu-er}
\end{equation}

In these elasticities, Result~\ref{res:welfare-bias} reads as follows.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(i)] overestimation iff $\varepsilon_r(p)<0$;
\item[(ii)] underestimation iff $\varepsilon_r(p)>0$ and either $\mu_p^0\geq 0$,
or both $\mu_p^0<0$ and $|\varepsilon_S|< -\varepsilon_D$;
\item[(iii)] overestimation with wrong sign (predicted loss, actual gain)
iff $\mu_p^0<0$ and $|\varepsilon_S|>-\varepsilon_D$.
\end{enumerate}
\begin{proof}
By Result~\ref{res:welfare-bias}, overestimation with $0<\Lambda<1$ is
$r'<0$, underestimation is $r'>0$ with $\rho_c>0$, and overestimation with
wrong sign is $r'>0$ with $\rho_c<0$.
Since $\varepsilon_r$ has the same sign as $r'$, write these in
$\varepsilon_r$.  For~(i), $\varepsilon_r<0$ forces $\mu_p^0>1-r>0$ and hence
$\rho_c>0$, so $0<\Lambda<1$.
When $\mu_p^0=0$ and $r(p)<1$, the derivative $r'(p)$ is positive,
$\varepsilon_S=0$, and $\rho_c=S'(\mu)/(-D'(p))>0$, so that case is in~(ii).
Result~\ref{res:negative-pt} translates the sign of $\rho_c$ into conditions
on $\mu_p^0$ and $|\varepsilon_S|\gtrless -\varepsilon_D$: with
$\varepsilon_r>0$ this is~(ii), and $\rho_c<0$ is~(iii).
\end{proof}
