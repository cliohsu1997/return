abstract
In this paper, we address the problem in which a manufacturer in a supply chain supplies a product to
two competing retailers. Both retailers, facing customer returns, must decide on prices for the product.
The manufacturer is a Stackelberg leader in the supply chain and the two retailers decide prices after the
manufacturer announces the wholesale price. One retailer is well established, offering a full-refund
policy, while the other retailer is a new entrant into the market place. The new entrant retailer needs to
decide whether to implement a full-refund policy or a no-refund policy, in order to compete with the
well-established retailer under two games: Nash game and Stackelberg game. We show that customer
returns policies can be used as competing marketing strategies. Numerical examples are included to
illustrate the major results of the paper. The analysis generates insights about how the degree to which
the customer accepts the no-refund product affects the new entrant retailer ′s choice of customer returns
policies in a competitive market.

1. Introduction
Handling customer returns has become critical as the volume
of products ﬂowing back from customers increases ( Guide et al.,
2006). Using survey data, the National Retail Federation reports
the value of products being returned as around an estimated $194
billion, or 8.12% of total sales, in 2010. Toktay (2003) states that
customer return rates range from 5% to 9% of sales for most
retailers. Mostard and Teunter (2006) report that for catalog
retailers, return rates on fashion items “are generally around
35– 40%, but can be as high as 75% ” of sales. When product returns
occur, handling the returned merchandise can impose substantial
costs on retailers. This has a substantive impact on retailer pro ﬁt-
ability, because returned products are used products to some
extent and are expected to have lower value than new products.
Chen and Bell (2009) show that a retailer who fails to account for
customer returns in price and ordering decisions will suffer
excessive inventory and a signi ﬁcant drop in pro ﬁtability. In fact,
Lawton (2008) points out that it is estimated that the U.S.
electronics industry spent $13.8 billion to repackage, restock, and
resell returned products in 2007 . Among returned products, only
about 5% of returns were because a product was truly defective.
Instead, most consumers reject products for other reasons, such as
the device being too confusing to use. Grimaldi (2008) notes that it
is estimated that managing product returns costs U.S. companies
over $200 billion annually.
Usually, a retailer receives returned products from customers
through a speciﬁed customer returns policy. In practice, there is no
law that requires any retail merchant to have a speci ﬁc refund,
credit or exchange policy. Customer returns policies can range
from “100% money back guarantees ” to “no refunds or exchanges ”
whatsoever. Several studies have examined how customer returns
policies affect the customer purchase and return decision, and the
consequences for retailers ' proﬁts (for example, Che, 1996; Davis
et al., 1995, 1998). In practice, many retail stores in North America
implement a liberal customer returns policy, because refusing to
accept the returned product is viewed as risking offending a
customer and losing a stream of revenue from repeated sales
(Davis et al., 1995 ). The Hudson 's Bay Company, Sears, and
Wal-Mart, for example, offer full refund policies for most of the
products they sell. For customers, of course, a full-refund customer
returns policy can eliminate the risk of having to keep an
unwanted product.
Some retailers, however, do not allow customer to return
products. Many retailers conduct periodic “sales” with an “all sales
are ﬁ
nal” statement (essentially, the no-refund case), implying that
the price mark-down is accompanied by a loss of product return
ability. It is common that Wal-Mart and Hudson 's Bay do not
provide refunds for food or fashion product effectively disallowing
the return of these products. When retailers provide no-refund
policies, they can save costs of having to keep returned product
and costs that are associated with handling customer returns.

The customers, on the other hand, will take the risk of keeping
mismatched product.
As compared to products with a full-refund policy, customers
perceive the same products sold with a no-refund policy as being
of lower value, due to the risk of having to keep the unwanted
products ( Heiman et al., 2005 ). In practice, some retailers offer a
full-refund policy to customers on products sold at a higher price
in regular stores, while offering no-returns policy on ﬁnal sales at a
lower price in “factory outlets. ” Davis et al. (1995) provide a
rationale for higher prices for returnable products: “money back
guarantees may allow the retailer to charge higher prices because
the reduction in the risk from the products being a poor match
with their tastes may increase a consumer 's willingness to pay. ”
In this paper, we model that customers perceived values are low
for the product with a no-refund policy and are high for the
product with a full-refund policy.
In this paper, we examine how a new entrant retailer can use a
full-refund returns policy or a no-refund returns policy to compete
with an established retailer who offers a full-refund customer
returns policy. The existing literature suggests that in a duopoly
setting money-back guarantees (MBGs) can be utilized by ﬁrms
with a low likelihood of returns to signal product quality to unin-
formed customers (for example, Moorthy and Srinivasan, 1995 ;
McWilliams, 2012). McWilliams (2012) shows that MBGs can help
the low-quality retailer in a duopoly model. In addition, some
products, such as durable products, have a relatively stable returns
rate, due to the nature of the products, suggesting that their returns
have little relation to the brand of the retailer. These previous
studies, however, do not include the conditions under which a
retailer can use customer returns policies as a means of competition
with other retailers. In our paper, we will identify these conditions.
2. Literature review
In the literature, returns are typically assumed to be a propor-
tion of product sold (sales), which implies that if more items are
sold, more products will be returned from customers (for example,
Vlachos and Dekker, 2003 ). To mitigate the negative impact of
customer returns on the retailer 'sp r o ﬁt, some research has
factored customer returns into pricing and ordering decisions.
Based on the assumptions that a ﬁxed percentage of sold products
will be returned and that products can be resold at most once in a
single period, Vlachos and Dekker (2003) investigate optimization
of order quantities for a newsvendor-style problem in which the
retail price is exogenous. Chen and Bell (2009) examine the impact
of customer returns on a ﬁrm's pricing and ordering decisions as
well as on the ﬁrm'sp r oﬁt. Xiao et al. (2010) consider a manu-
facturer and a retailer supply chain where the retailer faces
customer returns. They focus on the coordination mechanism in
a supply chain considering product returns. Su (2009) studies the
impact of returns polices (full-refund and partial-refund) on
supply chain performance and proposes a mechanism to coordi-
nate the supply chain in the presence of customer returns.
Shulman et al. (2009) describe how consumer purchase and return
decisions are affected by a seller 's pricing and restocking fee policy.
They identify conditions under which it is (or is not) optimal to
provide product ﬁt information to consumers. None of these
papers considers how a retailer can use different returns policies
as a competitive marketing strategy.
Although accepting customer returns reduces the retailer 's
proﬁt, it provides a valuable service to customers. Not accepting
customer returns saves the retailer expenditure, but customers
take the risk of keeping unwanted product after the purchase.
Offering different customer returns policies allows retailers to
differentiate themselves and discriminate prices, such that the
retailer can charge a higher price for a product that allows for
return but a lower price for the same product without the
possibility of return. Customers also have different preferences
on customer returns policies. Some customers, who are price
sensitive, prefer a low price at the expense of a no-refund policy,
while other customers prefer a liberal return policy, even if the
product price is higher. Heiman et al. (2005) show empirically that
a product with a more lenient customer returns policy results in a
higher value, as perceived by customers. Chen and Bell (2012)
consider the options of a retailer selling product through a
returnable channel, a non-returnable channel, or a dual channel
consisting of a returnable channel and a nonreturnable channel.
They show that using different returns policies to segment a
market and differentiate prices is a good way to enhance the
ﬁrm's pro ﬁt, while serving distinct customer bases with various
needs and purchase preferences. Making use of the results from
the empirical study ( Heiman et al., 2005 ) and as in Chen and Bell
(2012), in this paper, we suggest that customers perceive a higher
value for the product with a full-refund policy and a lower value
for the product with a no-refund policy. We investigate how a new
entrant retailer can implement a full-refund customer returns
policy or a no-refund customer returns policy to compete with an
established retailer offering a full-refund customer returns policy,
in a supply chain setting.

on price. The nature of this dependence, however, varies from model
to model (for example, Savaskan and Van Wassenhove, 2006 ).

Here, we will examine the conditions under which a new

Therefore, the paper contributes to the literature by examining
the impact of customer returns, returns policies, and customer

also generates new insights into the consideration of customer
returns policies for a new entrant in a competitive market.

3. Model framework and assumptions
In this paper, we consider a supply chain in which a manufacturer
supplies a product to two competing retailers. Both retailers, facing
customer returns, must set prices for the product. The manufacturer is
a Stackelberg leader in the supply chain and the two retailers decide
prices after the manufacturer announces the wholesale price. One
retailer is established, and offers a full-refund returns policy, while the
other retailer is a new entrant. The new entrant retailer needs to
decide which customer returns policy, full-refund or no-refund, should
be implemented as a marketing strategy to compete with the
established retailer. In practice, retailers offer various customer returns
policies, including full-refund, partial refund, or no-refund, to balance
customer satisfaction and the loss resulting from customer returns.
On the other hand, customers have different purchasing behaviors
based on familiarity with the product, sensitivity to the product'sp r i c e ,
and probability of returning the product (Chen and Bell, 2012). In this
paper, we use the framework of Hotelling (1929),b e c a u s eo fi t s
analytical tractability, considering two retailers who are located at a
certain distance from each other, which is normalized to one unit.
They may have different marketing power. We examine how a new
entrant retailer can compete with the established retailer by using
different customer returns policies.
Let two retailers be at the two ends of a unit length, as shown
in Fig. 1 . We assume that the customer 's transportation cost is t

retailer 1 incurs a transportation cost of xt if he purchases the

The manufacturer, as a Stackberg leader, should determine the
wholesale price (w) in anticipation of the retailers' selling prices, given
ap r o d u c t i o nc o s to fc per unit. After the manufacturer announcesw,
retailers decide selling prices. Without loss of generality, we assume
that in a local market, retailer 1 is an established retailer offering a
full-refund customer returns policy to its customer. Retailer 2 is a new
entrant, needing to decide which customer returns policy, a full-
refund or a no-refund policy, should be implemented to compete with
retailer 1. In reality, there are two different possible situations for the
new entrant. For example, a well-known brand retailer (for example,
Sears) will open a new store to compete with an existing retailer (for
example, Wal-Mart) in a local market. In such a case, the retailer'ss i z e
is competitive to that of the existing retailer. The new entrant can
compete with the existing retailer in a Nash game. In the literature,
several papers model the new entrant to compete with the existing
competitors in a Nash game (for example, Xie and Sirbu, 1995; Shiau
and Michalek, 2009; Choi et al., 1990). On the other hand, if the new
entrant is a retailer with such as a small size in business, it can be a
pricing follower in the market to compete with the existing retailer.
Such case is likely a common case in business (for example, Steiner,
2010; Ginevičius and Krivka, 2008). Several papers discuss both games
for the comparison purpose (for example, Xie and Sirbu, 1995; Choi
et al., 1990). Since both games are possible in practice, in this paper, we
thus consider two equilibrium games – Nash equilibrium and Stack-
elberg equilibrium– between the two retailers in deciding their retail
prices. In a Nash equilibrium (denoted with a superscript N), both
retailers have equal marketing powers, representing the case that two
retailers are competitive. In a Stackelberg equilibrium (denoted with a
superscript S), retailer 1 is the Stackelberg leader, who has an
established business or has a large size in business, while retailer
2 is a follower who might have a small size in business.

Providing a customer returns policy is usually regarded as a
service to customers. This research, however, provides new insights
for competing retailers facing customer returns in a supply chain.
We show that implementing different customer returns policies can
be used as a competitive strategy in the market to enhance a
retailer'sp r o ﬁt, while meeting the needs of distinct customer
groups. Speciﬁcally, we indentify the boundary conditions for when
the new entrant retailer should use a full-refund policy or a no-
refund policy to compete with an established retailer.
We show that, using a no-refund policy is a good option for the
new entrant retailer when the transportation cost is sufﬁciently high
(i.e., the market is partially-covered), only when the degree to which
the customer accepts the no-refund product is higher than a thresh-
old value. In addition, there exists a range of customer acceptance of
the no-refund productðθR1 o θ oθR2Þ such that both retailers, as well
as the manufacturer, are better off if new entrant retailer offers a full-
refund policy to compete with the established retailer.
We also show when the transportation cost is suf ﬁciently low
(i.e., the market is fully-covered), if the new entrant retailer
decides to use a full-refund policy to compete with the established
retailer, both retailers ' pricing decisions are independent of cus-
tomer returns rate and net salvage value.
The present research also has implications for pricing based on
type of product. For example, our results suggest that when a new
or unfamiliar product is marketed in the face of uncertain
customer expectations (the lower θ case), the new entrant retailer
should use a full-refund policy and set a higher retail price. If the
product is mature and the uncertainty of meeting the customer 's
expectations is lower (the higher θ case), selling the product using
a no-refund policy is a competitive strategy for the new entrant
retailer, allowing competition with the established retailer. In this
way, the new entrant retailer can attract customers who are
familiar with the product but prefer a lower price.
The research shows that it is critical for the new entrant retailer
competing with an established retailer to understand the degree
to which the customer accepts the no-refund product ( θ) before
deciding on a customer returns policy. Comprehensive marketing
research is needed to understand the customer 's preference (a low
price with a no-refund policy vs. a high price with a full-refund
policy) so that the new entrant retailer can obtain a relatively
precise measure of the degree to which the customer accepts the
no-refund product.
This research also shows that competing using customer
returns policies can be a pro ﬁt enhancement strategy and high-
lights the need for the supply chain that is facing customer returns
to take customer returns into consideration in pricing decisions.
In this paper, we raise some notes of caution for the manu-
facturer; the decision of the new entrant retailer on customer
returns policies and pricing games may not bene ﬁt the manufac-
turer. The manufacturer might need a side contract instead of the
wholesale price only contract to incentivize the new entrant
retailer, so that both can bene ﬁt from the decision of the new
entrant retailer. This situation should be addressed in future
research.
